# 14. FORCE IMPRESSIONS IN WARDE’S ROOM

These are proposed narrative manifestations of the location, not automatic new functions granted to a purchased Force power.

Use the character’s established abilities and the table’s existing treatment of Force echoes.

## 14.1 Baseline impression

A character already sensitive to the palace’s residue may perceive:

> Weariness without rest.
>
> Attention turning back to the same thought.
>
> A momentary sense that every interruption might be dangerous.

Ask:

> “Do you stay with that impression, or step away?”

## 14.2 If they deliberately remain

Offer fragments rather than a complete film.

### Fragment A — The cup

The impulse to reach for it, followed by the realization it is empty.

### Fragment B — The doorway

Awareness of movement outside.

For a moment, the approach feels like a threat.

The impression contains no evidence that the person outside intended harm.

### Fragment C — The board

A line of possibilities seems to narrow because the observer keeps returning to the same branch.

This is an emotional representation, not a reliable schematic of actual alternate timelines.

## 14.3 What the echo should not do

- Reveal the entire Level 16 confrontation.
- Identify the remaining presence automatically.
- Confirm every suspicion Warde held.
- Force a PC to attack a companion.
- Assign Conflict merely for witnessing another person’s emotions.
- Require the party to agree that Warde was redeemable.

## 14.4 Grounding options

Characters may:

- Speak their own names.
- Read the factual ledger entry aloud.
- Step into the corridor.
- Ask a companion to describe something present and ordinary.
- Focus on the stone, cup, or table as physical objects.
- Stop investigating.

No roll is required to choose to disengage from this optional impression.

## 14.5 Sigg’s possible responses

Use only one if the players need an opening.

### After the board

> “He wrote that there were other versions.”

### After the exhaustion impression

> “I believe he was exhausted. I still want to know what he chose.”

### If someone calls him a monster

> “Then we still need to understand how he got here.”

### If someone excuses him completely

> “The people across the corridor were tired too.”

### If someone thinks the apology was meant for them

> “Maybe. I don’t think we can know that from these words.”

These lines invite discussion. They do not establish Sigg as the final authority.

# 3. THE LEVEL AS A REAL PLACE

## 3.1 What Level 15 used to do

Level 15 handled the transition between public ceremony and closed negotiations.

Visitors arrived through the Grand Atrium. Reception recorded their presence and directed them into preparation suites. Staff distributed briefing materials, checked translation equipment, adjusted hospitality arrangements, and coordinated the conference level below.

The architecture expresses two different kinds of work:

**The ceremonial route:**

Wide corridors, formal sightlines, comfortable waiting spaces, immaculate public finishes.

**The service route:**

Narrower passages, replaceable panels, drainage channels, tool access, delivery hatches, and places where employees could sit without being watched by delegates.

The service route explains how the ceremonial route functioned.

## 3.2 Why so much survives

Different materials survived differently.

- Sealed chemical cartridges remained dormant until activated.
- Physical ledgers survived in a dry, sheltered reception alcove.
- Exterior fabrics rotted; protected folded papers survived unevenly.
- Moisture damaged writing near outer walls.
- Dark terminals retained only limited local records.
- Cabinet doors protected their contents from drifting dust.
- Staff repairs outlasted some expensive decorative fittings.

Do not describe every object as pristine or every system as broken.

A palace becomes believable when damage follows its construction.

## 3.3 What is happening now

The party’s arrival changes the level.

- Dormant lights begin consuming their remaining cartridges.
- Old relay mechanisms transmit local signals.
- A failing drain starts carrying newly released condensation.
- A restored terminal adds load to an unreliable electrical bus.
- Fresh footprints cross spaces that have been undisturbed for years.
- If connected security droids survive below, certain signals may attract attention.

The palace is responsive through machinery and environment. It does not need to be secretly conscious.

## 3.4 Small signs of ordinary life

Use one or two per room.

| Detail | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| A polished indentation beneath the reception desk | Someone repeatedly hooked a foot around the same support while working. |
| A drawer labeled “VISITOR STYLUSES” containing mismatched pens | Official presentation depended on ordinary improvisation. |
| A cup marked “NOT FOR SERVICE” | A staff member wanted one object to remain theirs. |
| A replacement chair foot cut from darker material | The room remained presentable because someone repaired it. |
| A small tally of shifts scratched inside a service cupboard | Work continued in a place visitors never noticed. |
| A translator earpiece wrapped for repair | Delegates depended on imperfect equipment and patient technicians. |
| A sleeve protector beside handwritten forms | Someone spent long hours copying records. |
| A folded cushion behind a console | A person rested here without a proper break room. |
| A joke crossed out on a staff notice | The staff disagreed about what was funny. |
| Two versions of a menu | Someone remembered that an important guest could not eat the first one. |

These details do not all become quests. Their value is that the world contains lives beyond the investigation.

# Session 4 — Storm Shelter and the Wanderer: Full GM Walkthrough

## Opening — trapped together

Open with the storm making departure impossible. Red lightning walks across the canyon rim without thunder, and sheets of charged dust turn the open ground into a lethal choice. The old man has a stone shelter large enough for everyone but not large enough for privacy.

> The stranger braces his cane against the door until the last person is inside. Red light pulses through cracks in the stone. He passes a flask without asking who needs it most. “Introductions can wait,” he says. “Breathing cannot.”

The water is real and safe. Let the party test it. He does not take offense, but notices who drinks first and who gives their share away.

The shelter contains a heater, repaired scavenged tools, a sleeping mat, carefully stacked stones, and years of tiny practical improvements. This is not a temporary camp. Anyone who examines it learns that the Wanderer has lived in the region for decades. He avoids direct lies: he calls himself a seeker, says he came for answers, and admits he was stranded “some years ago.”

## The first conversation

Run this as a conversation with pauses, not an interrogation table. The Wanderer is friendly, brief, and sometimes distracted by memories. He knows the characters’ names or motivations imperfectly through clouded foresight. When he uses a name nobody gave him, let the silence sit.

Useful answers in his voice:

- **Who are you?** “A seeker of truth. I found more solitude than truth, but one learns to work with what is available.”
- **How did you know we were coming?** “Time reveals secrets, including those we keep from ourselves. It is less considerate about the order.”
- **Are you alone?** “I am the only living soul here. That is not the same as being alone.”
- **What is this world?** “You have a name for it. Names are useful until they begin telling us what we are allowed to see.”
- **Do you know Warde?** He waits before answering: “I knew a wounded man who used that name.”

The gatekeeper questions him precisely. Let it ask about Warde’s posture, injuries, speech habits, and lightsaber hand. The old man answers with technically true details that feel like memory rather than observation. The gatekeeper fails to recognize him and grows unsettled. It finally says privately, “He knows facts I contain but did not volunteer. Either he met Warde, or he has studied us.”

## Warde’s invented death

When trust or pressure reaches the right point, the Wanderer tells his story. Warde arrived gravely wounded. The hermit made him comfortable. Before dying, Warde predicted that the party would come. Unable to dig a grave in the hard ground, the hermit carried him to an old tomb.

Do not deliver this as flawless exposition. He stops at the word “dying,” corrects a date, and describes carrying the body with a strength his current frailty cannot explain. A medically trained character notices that his account of the wounds is exact. A perceptive character notices that he never directly says, “I watched him die.”

If confronted, he smiles:

> “Do I look like a Jedi Knight to you? I would be flattered if the comparison did not sound so exhausting.”

Allow an opposed Discipline-versus-Deception test only after players identify what they are testing. Moraband aids his concealment. Success should not print his name over his head; it reveals that the deception protects grief, fear, and anticipation rather than a plan to rob them. Triumph catches him looking at the holocron as someone might look at a younger self. Failure still leaves visible contradictions.

## The storm’s second front

Halfway through the conversation, the storm creates a practical crisis. Charged dust begins entering through a broken vent, a companion outside loses contact, or the shelter’s stone roof shifts. Warde helps without displaying his full power. He predicts where debris will fall, catches a weight a frail man should not catch, or is already moving before an alarm sounds.

This event lets the party choose cooperation, exploitation, or suspicion. If they protect him or one another, Warde Trust rises. If they arrange an “accident” to test him, warn that harming a possibly innocent old man carries Conflict. If the pursuers are present, give one of them a private exchange with him. Hasar may stare and say, “I know your voice.” He replies, “Then you know how unreliable memory becomes when fed hatred.”

## The stone garden after the storm

When the lightning passes, Warde leads them outside. Water has collected in shallow black basins between standing stones. The stones are arranged like branching paths. Several arrangements resemble decisions the party made earlier: one stone separated at the oasis, two facing each other at the landing site, one broken beside a circle representing the camp.

Warde claims he moves them while meditating. He will not say whether he arranged these before the party arrived. Let characters walk the paths, rearrange stones, or refuse the exercise. If someone deliberately changes the ending of a stone pattern, Warde becomes visibly uncomfortable. This is direct evidence that he fears mutable futures.

He asks each character one question:

- “What decision do you most wish had been inevitable?”
- “If a vision showed you betraying these people, when would you tell them?”
- “What proof would convince you that your greatest fear was only fear?”
- “If Warde lives, do you want justice, knowledge, or permission?”

Play his reactions honestly. Compassion unsettles him. Certainty relaxes him, even when hostile, because it supports his worldview. A character who admits uncertainty but still chooses a principle gives him the first model of courage he has seen in years.

## The offer

Warde eventually says he knows the tomb’s location and can guide them. He calls it only a few hours away and refuses to state his price:

> “I can take you to what remains of him. The desert is dangerous, but not complicated.” He leans on the cane and smiles. “I will ask one small thing when we arrive. If I name it now, you will mistake anticipation for choice.”

The party may accept, reject, place conditions on him, restrain him, or secretly follow. All routes continue.

- **Accept:** he departs at first light and walks more strongly than before.
- **Reject:** he gives a true direction, then follows at a distance because his vision requires the confrontation.
- **Restrain:** he allows it briefly, then demonstrates that he could have escaped. This lowers trust but does not force combat.
- **Expose him:** if they present strong evidence, he does not confess. He says, “If I were Warde, would that change what you intend to do?” The journey becomes tense and the reveal may occur before the palace.
- **Attack:** he defends, disarms, and retreats toward the palace. Move the story into a pursuit without discarding the conversation’s evidence.

## Closing image

End with the old man walking ahead beneath the red sky. His cane strikes the ground in an irregular rhythm. After several minutes, a character realizes the rhythm exactly matches the damaged cockpit warning from Warde’s fighter.

Record who trusted him, who threatened him, what each character said they wanted from Warde, and whether anyone changed a stone-path ending. Those details determine his tone and social resistance in the finale.

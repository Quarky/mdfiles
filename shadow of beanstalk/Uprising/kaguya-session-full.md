OPENING — THE TUBE-LEV

04:40. The Docklands transit hub departure board flickered from blank to live.
Nobody announced it. It just changed. Everyone in the waiting area looked at it
the same way — the way people look at something they don't trust but are going to use.

Dante's car is the fourth of the morning. It's full.

The ride takes twenty-two minutes. The car has twenty-six people in it.
Eight of them are Sigg's. Dante knows which eight because of how they sit —
not together, deliberately not together, but with the same quality of stillness
that people have when they have made a decision and are waiting for the moment
to act on it.

The other eighteen are workers, service staff, a maintenance contractor
with a heavy case of equipment, and a Melange logistics manager who is
reviewing files on a PAD and has not looked up since the doors closed.

At the sixth minute a woman across the aisle — late fifties, Filipina,
Melange contractor badge turned face-down on her jacket —
says to no one in particular:

  "They turned it back on so they know where we are."

Nobody responds. But three people shift in their seats.

At the fourteenth minute the crater rim is visible through the scratched porthole.
Kaguya from the outside looks like it always looks.
The cluster of dome lights. The warm glow of a place that spent enormous money
trying to feel welcoming.

Nobody on this tube-lev has seen what it is about to become.
The Melange logistics manager is still reading his files.

At minute twenty-two the doors open.
The woman with the face-down badge stands.
Straightens her jacket.
Turns the badge face-up.
She walks out like she belongs here. Because she does.

05:00 — OUTER RING, KAGUYA
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The outer ring at 05:00 is the tourist infrastructure in its early-morning state.
Moving walkways running empty. StarScape retail shutters up, window displays lit
by automated systems. The stale particular quiet of a large space before people fill it.

A Haas-Bioroid maintenance crew works on a walkway junction. Three bioroids
in facility coveralls, one human supervisor reading a PAD without watching them.
The bioroids work in perfect coordinated silence. One has a small scratch
on its left forearm that catches the light as it reaches past Dante.

He has seen that kind of scratch before.

Two Globalsec guards at the arterial junction who would not normally be here
at 05:00 are here at 05:00. Not stopping anyone. Watching.
One of them — young, Earth-born, still adjusting to the gravity —
is watching the crowd with the attention of someone told to look for something
specific but not told what. He has never done this before.
Neither has anyone else in this building.

Dante is in the lower service corridor when it happens above him.
He hears it as a change in the ambient noise —
the normal murmur of a spaceport morning replaced by something
that has more voices in it, more direction. Not chaos. Intention.

The atrium is the size of a small dome. High ceiling. Moving walkway access
on three levels. A fountain in the center that runs on recycled water
and exists to signal that water is not a resource concern here —
the most expensive lie Kaguya tells.

At 06:30 the fountain is turned off. Not by Globalsec.
By a worker named Amara who knows where the shutoff valve is
because she installed it eighteen months ago as a Weyland subcontractor
and was paid sixty percent of the contracted rate
because the work was deemed "incomplete" despite being complete.

She turns off the fountain. She sits down next to it.
Ninety-three people sit down with her.

The tourists in the atrium do not run. Some film it on PADs.
One — a teenager from São Paulo, first trip off-Earth —
asks Amara what's happening. Amara tells her plainly.
The teenager sits down for four minutes.
Until her mother finds her and pulls her away.
She looks back over her shoulder as she's pulled.
This is the frame of the recording that will matter most.
It is not the frame she intended.

WHO THEY ARE AND HOW THEY FIGHT
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

What arrives at Kaguya are U.S. Air Force and Marine rapid deployment units.
They trained specifically for lunar and zero-G combat —
the famous Asphyxiation Alley runs at their Nevada simulation facility,
where you fight your way through darkness with one tank of O2
and you either get out or you understand what fighting in space actually means.

They are some of the best-prepared soldiers in the world for this environment.
They have never used that preparation in an actual lunar combat situation.
Nobody alive has. They are doing something genuinely new.

This creates a specific quality of behavior that Sigg's experienced fighters notice:
the military moves with almost too much precision — every action deliberate,
every position covered before the next move, a little slower than an experienced
combatant in a familiar environment would be. They are applying doctrine
to a situation their doctrine was designed for but never tested in.

Their doctrine is good. The slight over-deliberateness creates windows
that a force with facility knowledge can exploit — not against their weapons,
not against their armor, but against their timing.

THE MILITARY'S THREE ADVANTAGES:
  1. Sealed hardsuits — immune to decompression, thin air, atmospheric manipulation.
     Everything the Loonies can do to the environment with atmo overrides
     and pressure edits does nothing to soldiers in fully sealed suits.
  2. Weapons that punch through the armor the workers bought. The NEXT CG44
     Gauss Rifle and the Argus 180R Laser Rifle at military grade are different
     categories from what Globalsec carries. The mercenaries know this.
     The workers are about to learn it.
  3. Coordinated EWS support. The Electronic Warfare Service operators
     at Armstrong Base are degrading Kaguya's networks in real time,
     closing ICE gaps faster than they would close on their own.
     Everything Dante is running is harder in Phase 2 than it was in Phase 1.

THE WORKERS' ONE ADVANTAGE:
  They built this place. They know where every maintenance hatch goes.
  They know which corridor sections have degraded atmo seals —
  not because it's in any tactical document but because they filed the work orders.
  They know which checkpoints Globalsec has never properly staffed
  because the staffing schedule hasn't been updated since a manager retired.
  They know that the ventilation stack between Dome Beta and Ring 2
  creates a blind spot in the camera coverage that the security system
  has documented as a known issue for four months without fixing.
  They know these things the way you know your own house in the dark.

─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
THE ENTRY — HOW THE MILITARY COMES IN
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

They don't come through the tube-lev. They don't come through the Beanstalk.
They come from orbit, through the crater's open sky, on the far side
from the civilian structures. Four dropships running dark — minimal heat signature,
no running lights, coming in steep and fast on the crater's southeast quadrant.

The landing is hard by civilian standards and routine by military ones.
The crater floor on the southeast side is below the dome cluster's sightlines —
the terrain of the crater rim blocks the view from inside Kaguya.
Nobody inside sees them land. They hear it as the building changing timbre.

VASORA'S PLAN: The military force splits into three elements immediately on landing.

ELEMENT 1 — OUTER CORDON:
  12 troopers + 2 exosuit pilots seal the crater rim.
  The private landing pads in Ring 4 are covered from outside.
  Nothing leaves Kaguya via the surface.
  The exosuit pilots carry NEXT CG70(H) Anti-Armour Heavy Gauss Rifles —
  not for people, for vehicles. If anyone tries to leave in a hopper,
  the hopper has a bad day. This is the reason Li Wei's hopper window
  is exactly 90 seconds — after that the exosuits have a clear shot.

ELEMENT 2 — INNER PUSH:
  24 troopers + Vasora advance through the outer ring from the southeast
  emergency airlock. They come in sealed, organized, two-by-two,
  clearing sections methodically. Laser rifles — Argus 180R and NEXT L12.
  No slugthrowers inside a pressurized dome. They know this without being told.
  The EWS drone goes forward in every corridor before they enter it.
  This is their first tactical pattern. Every corridor: drone first.
  If the drone returns intact: advance. If it takes fire: assess before advancing.

ELEMENT 3 — NETWORK SUPPRESSION:
  4 EWS operators at Armstrong Base, coordinating remotely.
  They are not at Kaguya physically. They are everywhere Kaguya's network goes.
  They close ICE gaps in real time, deploy RAIDER against active network intrusions,
  and monitor all communication frequencies for coordination between the Loony force.
  They are very good. They are also doing this for the first time in a live environment.
  Their simulations did not include a runner who has Outpost 14's network architecture
  memorized and knows how to find the gap between doctrine and practice.

THE TACTICAL ENCOUNTERS — HOW THE MILITARY FIGHTS
─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

ENCOUNTER: THE DRONE SCREEN

Every corridor the military advances through, they send the EWS combat drone first.
The drone is physically present, not just a network construct —
it enters the corridor, scans with full sensor array (heat, motion, acoustic, visual),
transmits, and waits for the all-clear before the troopers follow.

What this looks like in practice: the drone rounds a corner with a sound
like a large insect — deliberate, unhurried — and hovers at head height,
its amber light pulsing. It is looking at everything. It is telling everything
to the EWS operators who are telling Vasora.

What this means for the Loony fighters: every position they hold,
the military knows exactly what is in it before they commit to engaging.
The first time this happens, three of Sigg's fighters at a junction
watch a drone enter, scan them, and back out. Then silence for ninety seconds.
Then the troopers come around the corner in a formation that already knows
exactly where each fighter is standing.

The second time: Sera says "Don't fire on the drone. Let it see us.
We already moved." The drone enters, scans, transmits a position
that is forty seconds out of date. The troopers advance on the positions
the drone reported. The positions are empty. The Loony fighters
are behind them through a maintenance hatch that no tactical document
lists as a viable route because it was partially sealed three years ago
and the seal failed eight months ago and nobody updated the document.

The drone screen works perfectly until it encounters people
who know which parts of the building aren't in any document.

ENCOUNTER: THE COMPRESSED ADVANCE

The military's second tactical pattern: compress the Loony force
between two elements — the outer cordon pushing inward
and the inner push pushing outward. Meet in the middle.
Anyone in the middle gets processed or gets hurt.

This is textbook. It works in most situations.
In Kaguya it creates the compression zone —
the space between the elements where Loony fighters, civilians who couldn't
exit, and the occasional mercenary are all mixed together in the same corridors
that the military is trying to move through while minimizing civilian casualties.

Vasora's RoE says: detain over kill, do not fire into civilian concentrations.
Her troopers follow this. They are disciplined.
They are also having difficulty distinguishing fighters from workers
in the corridors where both are present, both are in similar clothing,
and some of the workers are carrying weapons while some of the fighters
are not actively wielding theirs.

This difficulty creates windows. Small ones. They close when the drone
gets a clean scan, but between scans there are seconds where the distinction
matters and the military's doctrine says to hold fire until it's clear.
The Loony fighters learn this is how it works approximately
in the third junction they hold. It buys them time they didn't expect to have.

ENCOUNTER: THE DECOMPRESSION EVENT

At minute 35 of Dante's run, a partial decompression occurs
in the connecting corridor between Dome Beta and Ring 1.
Not catastrophic — emergency seals activate and contain it
before anyone is in immediate danger of death.
But the 85% O2 level in the sealed section is noticeable within 10-12 minutes
for anyone without a sealed suit.

The military troopers advance through it without slowing.
Their suits are sealed.
The Loony workers in that section are not in sealed suits.
Their environmental hardsuits — the ones Sigg ordered first,
before any weapon — are somewhere else, on fighters at other positions.

The workers in the corridor have a problem the soldiers don't have.
The soldiers know this. Some of them stop and redirect civilians
toward the emergency surface hatch before they clear the section.
This slows their advance. This is also the right thing to do.
Vasora does not countermand it.

The cause of the decompression: EWS interference disrupting Kaguya's
atmo management network destabilized a pressure differential sensor
in section B-7, which caused an automated over-pressurization sequence
in the adjacent section, which caused the seal between B-7 and the
Ring 1 corridor to rupture at its weakest point.

This is documented in the EWS operators' logs as "incidental environmental effect."
It is also documented in Petra Vasquez's notes as "military action causing
civilian atmospheric hazard." Both of these are accurate descriptions
of the same event. History will argue about which framing is correct
for a long time after today.

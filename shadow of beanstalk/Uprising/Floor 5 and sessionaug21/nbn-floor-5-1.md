## DESCENT — THE MAGNETIC ELEVATOR

The magnetic elevator car is standard corporate specification — 2.5 by 2.5 meters, stainless steel interior, a handrail along the back wall, a small display above the door showing the current floor number and direction arrow. The car descends in near-silence. The only sounds are the faint hum of the magnetic propulsion system and the occasional soft click as floor indicators pass on the shaft display.

Descending from Floor 4 to Floor 5, three things happen:

**First:** The lighting changes. Floor 4 has the warm amber of NBN's corporate color scheme — the same amber as the lobby, the same amber as every NBN screen in Heinlein. The elevator car's interior lighting shifts from amber to cold white as it passes between floors. For approximately four seconds Dante is in transition between two different kinds of institutional certainty.

**Second:** The temperature drops. Not immediately — it begins as a suggestion, a coolness that the air on Floor 4's lower levels already carried. But in the shaft it becomes something deliberate, something with engineering behind it. By the time the car reaches Floor 5, Dante can feel it through his clothing. This is not passive cool. Something is working hard to make this floor as cold as it is.

**Third:** The sound changes. Floor 4's sounds — voices, the hum of occupied offices, the occasional system alert from the network rooms — disappear. Floor 5 produces its own sound: a deep, constant, layered mechanical hum that Dante feels in his sternum before he hears it with his ears. The cooling systems. Running continuously. They have been running for years. They produce a sound that is less like noise and more like pressure.

The car settles at Floor 5 with a faint magnetic click. The door slides open onto a short corridor ending at a pressure door. Beyond the pressure door: Floor 5.

Dante's breath is visible before the pressure door is open. The air is approximately minus eight degrees Celsius. This is not a malfunction. This is the specification.

## FLOOR 5 — PHYSICAL LAYOUT

Floor 5 is carved directly into the basalt. Not spray-sealed, not faced — the walls here are raw lunar rock, cut with precision tunneling equipment and left. The basalt is grey-black with faint mineral striations, smooth from the cutting process but not polished. It catches the overhead lighting at angles and returns it fractured.

The floor is the exception — poured plascrete, sealed, perfectly level. The ceiling is three meters up, irregular at the edges where the basalt wasn't perfectly flat. Cable management runs along the ceiling in heavy insulated conduit, the insulation a specific yellow that means cryogenic-rated. Everything here is rated for temperatures that would damage standard infrastructure.

Six sections separated by blast-rated pressure doors. Each door has a physical key panel and a secondary biometric scanner — biometrics offline due to the crisis backend deprioritization.

**SECTION A — THE PRIMARY SERVER CORRIDOR**
The main space. Four rows of server racks running the full length of the floor, north to south. This is where VX-ARCHIVE-7 lives.

**SECTION B — THE COOLING PLANT**
West side. The physical cooling infrastructure — compressors, refrigerant cycling, the massive thermal management systems that produce Floor 5's temperature. Loud. Brutally cold at minus fourteen. One significant NPC here.

**SECTION C — THE MONITORING ALCOVE**
Northeast corner, partially open to the main corridor. Eight screens showing automated status readouts. No human operator present — all monitoring staff are watching the crisis broadcast on Floor 3.

**SECTION D — THE DIAGNOSTIC ROOM**
Southeast corner, behind the floor's only fully locked door. A physical laboratory space used for hardware diagnostics and, less officially, for periodic maintenance of VX-ARCHIVE-7 by whoever holds FOUNDER credentials.

**SECTION E — THE STORAGE ANNEX**
Southwest corner, accessed through a secondary door from the cooling plant. Obsolete hardware, replacement parts, items that were never officially disposed of.

**SECTION F — THE NETWORK RELAY JUNCTION**
East wall, midway between the server corridor and the monitoring alcove. The hardwired connection point for the building's network infrastructure. Contains the physical termination point for the STATIC-BC uplink.

## SECTION A — THE PRIMARY SERVER CORRIDOR

Four rows of server racks, each row twelve meters long, the racks 2.1 meters tall. The rows are numbered on the ceiling in stenciled white paint: ROWS 1 through 4, north to south. Row 1 is nearest the elevator. Row 4 is at the far end, against the southern rock wall.

The cold here has texture. It moves in currents produced by the cooling systems drawing heat away from the racks. Walking between rows, Dante encounters cold that shifts and eddies — warmer in one step and colder in the next. The racks generate heat but the cooling is so aggressive that heat is captured before it reaches the air. A paradox: surrounded by hardware actively producing heat, the air remains at minus eight. The cooling systems are winning continuously, every second, by a significant margin.

The lighting is cold blue-white LEDs — the color temperature of efficiency. Each rack has its own indicator lights: green status, amber warning, red fault. At a glance: Row 1, all green. Row 2, mostly green, two amber. Row 3, all green. Row 4 — VX-ARCHIVE-7 and neighbors — mostly green, with one rack showing an unusual light pattern.

## SECTION B — THE COOLING PLANT

The pressure door is the heaviest on this floor — thermal-rated, designed to prevent heat from the machinery leaking into the server corridor. Pushing it open requires effort. The seal breaks with a sound like a breath exhaled.

The temperature on the other side is somehow colder than the server corridor. The machinery produces cold as a byproduct of producing more cold. The plant is approximately minus fourteen degrees Celsius.

The sound is enormous. Industrial-scale compressors — the size of vehicles — arranged in two parallel rows, connected by refrigerant piping insulated in yellow cryogenic wrapping. The pipes run through the walls into the server corridor. The pipes have frost on their outer surfaces despite the insulation. The cold is winning even against its own containment.

The space smells of refrigerant — a faint chemical sweetness at the back of the throat. Not dangerous at this concentration. Distinctive. The smell of a system that has been running for a long time without a full service.

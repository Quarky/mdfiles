## PHYSICAL REALITY — ESTABLISHING THE FLOOR

Before anything specific, the floor imposes itself physically. Run this as atmosphere before any investigation begins.

**THE COLD:** Minus eight. The kind of cold that requires infrastructure to maintain — active cooling systems fighting the server hardware's heat output continuously, every second, and winning. The cold has texture: it moves in currents between the rack rows as the cooling systems draw heat away. One step is colder than the last. The next is warmer. The floor breathes cold in a specific pattern that Dante learns within two minutes of walking the rows, if he pays attention.

**THE SOUND:** Four layers. Deepest: the compressors in the cooling plant beyond the west pressure door, a subsonic frequency felt more than heard. Second: the server racks themselves, a constant mid-register hum of a thousand processors. Third: the atmospheric management system cycling air through the conduits above. Fourth, periodic: the soft electronic click of status light changes on the racks, random but frequent, the floor's own Morse code that means nothing except that everything is running normally.

The four layers together produce something that is not quite silence and not quite noise. It is the sound of a system that is always on.

**THE LIGHT:** Cold blue-white LEDs. Not the warm amber of the floors above. Not the honest fluorescent of Floor 4's administrative corridor. This light is calibrated for machines, not people. It makes skin look wrong. It makes the basalt walls look like they are sweating. It catches the frost patterns on the older hardware at angles that the designers of this floor did not intend and could not have predicted.

**THE SMELL:** A cold metallic undertone — the refrigerant system, faintly chemical, at the threshold of perception. Underneath it: the specific smell of hardware that has been running continuously for years. Not dust. Not age. Something more neutral than either. The smell of a system that doesn't know what stopping feels like.

### THE HAAS-BIOROID — THE RECORDING PROBLEM

In Row 2, a Haas-Bioroid maintenance unit is performing a routine inspection of the amber indicators. Cold-environment chassis — thermal-insulated housing, slightly different proportions from the standard model. It works with the systematic precision of something that has run this sequence hundreds of times.

**THE RECORDING PROBLEM:**

The bioroid's optical sensors are recording. Local storage only. Scheduled upload: 22:00 HLT tonight. If Dante does nothing, his presence on Floor 5 today will be in the NBN maintenance record by this evening.

> **Computers Hard:** Wipe local storage without triggering an upload alert. **Computers Average:** Access the diagnostic port to read the log and understand what's been recorded. **Do nothing:** The footage uploads at 22:00. This matters later.

**THE MAINTENANCE LOG (via diagnostic port):**

Not just today's inspection. Three years of Floor 5 maintenance logs, stored locally as backup before upload. The log shows every anomaly the bioroid has flagged and had its anomaly report suppressed:

_Three years of the same entry._ The same flag. The same suppression. Someone set the review status to PENDING three years ago and never closed it. The bioroid has been filing the same flag, correctly, for three years. The flag has been pending review for three years.

This is how the building manages what it doesn't want to see: not by removing the record, but by leaving the record in an eternal pending state where no one is required to act on it.

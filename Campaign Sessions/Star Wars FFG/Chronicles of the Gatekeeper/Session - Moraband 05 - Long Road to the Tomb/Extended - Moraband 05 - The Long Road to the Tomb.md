# Session 5 — The Long Road to the Tomb: Full GM Walkthrough

**Campaign:** Chronicles of the Gatekeeper  
**System:** Star Wars FFG / Force and Destiny  
**Canonical status:** Repaired extended-session version, 2026-10-03  
**Repair type:** Preserves the strong v2.2.1 runtime walkthrough while adding unique world-motion, map-use, failure-forward, and carry-forward guidance. Shared boilerplate from the old scene toolkits is intentionally not duplicated here.

## Canonical runtime additions — world motion, consequences, and map handling

### What is happening without the PCs
Warde knows the route and is deciding how much to reveal. The environment becomes more monumental as the group nears the tomb/palace: the whispering dunes hide old tracks, the statues create long lines of observation, and rivals can use the same landmarks to close distance. Warde may deliberately choose a harder route if he thinks the party needs to see a particular site or avoid a pursuer.

### Run-time map notes
Use the journey as linked scenes rather than one travel montage. The dunes test communication and trust; the statues externalize history; the overlook gives everyone a chance to reassess the destination; the approach should make the palace feel like a chosen threshold rather than the next map in a queue.

### Failure forward
Travel failures produce exposure, fatigue, delay, separation, or rival positioning. They do not erase the route. If the party distrusts Warde and navigates independently, allow it; the cost is uncertainty and time, not GM veto.

### Carry-forward emphasis
Record whether Warde is walking as guide, prisoner, ally, or tolerated stranger by the time the palace comes into view.

## Opening — following a man who knows the ending

Begin in motion. The Wanderer sets a slow pace but never checks direction. The landscape offers no trail. Ask where each character walks relative to him and who watches the rear.

> The old man’s cane leaves a line of small circles in the dust. Dunes erase your own tracks within minutes, but his remain untouched behind him. When anyone looks directly at them, they appear ordinary again.

The purpose of this session is not to hide that the Wanderer is Warde at all costs. It is to let the party accumulate a case, decide what that truth means, and prove through action that prediction does not remove choice.

## The whispering dunes

Warde answers questions while walking, but rarely initiates speech. Use the journey to revisit the campaign. He asks what became of people affected by his past decisions. If told about mercy, reform, or an outcome nobody predicted, he searches for a reason it was secretly inevitable. If told about loss, he accepts it too quickly as proof.

Give him lines that expose the argument beneath the mystery:

> “A choice can feel free and still be the only choice a person like you would make.”

> “Hope is often hindsight wearing ceremonial robes.”

> “If you prove my story false, you have not proved the future open. You have only proved I told a poor story.”

During the first hour, the dunes whisper possible endings: the party carrying the crystal over Warde’s body, Hasar executing him, the gatekeeper calling the characters murderers, or Warde leaving peacefully. Different characters hear different endings. Warde hears all of them and becomes irritable.

A storm-buried supply cache provides a practical interlude. It contains old water reclamation parts, climbing line, and a sealed medpac. Warde insists the cache is empty because he remembers finding it empty years ago. The characters open it and prove him wrong. This is small, physical evidence that conditions change. Preserve the item they choose to take; it can matter in the palace.

## Statues of the fallen

The route passes between colossal statues of Sith rulers. Most have fallen in the same direction, as though bowing toward a distant structure. One remains upright. Its face resembles whichever party member is carrying the holocron—not perfectly, but enough to disturb everyone.

Warde says, “It did not look like that yesterday.” This is the first unsolicited admission that his certainty fails.

The path divides. The obvious route passes under the standing statue, where unstable stone threatens collapse. A longer route crosses exposed ground visible to pursuers. A third route can be made by toppling the statue deliberately, which risks awakening a dark relic chamber beneath it.

Let the party solve the crossing. During the crisis, Warde’s cane snaps or is lost. He forgets to stoop afterward. If someone falls, he uses the Force openly to save them before remembering his disguise. He does not apologize. He asks, “Would you have preferred the consistency of my lie?”

This is the cleanest point for an early identity confrontation. If the party names him, he can admit only: “The man you seek and the man guiding you share responsibility.” He still leads them onward because he believes the final scene must occur at the palace.

## The overlook and rival reckoning

At the tomb-valley overlook, reveal multiple ruined complexes below and the palace in the far distance. Give the party time to stop. This is also where unresolved pursuers catch up or make contact.

If rivals are enemies, they demand the guide and holocron. Warde does not intervene immediately; he watches what the PCs do with an enemy when violence is easy. If the party offers mercy, negotiation, or rescue from the landscape, Warde’s certainty weakens. If they execute helpless opponents, his vision hardens.

If Hasar is allied, she recognizes him here. Her accusation should be personal and specific:

> “Stand straight, Warde. I want them to see the man whose ghost they have been defending.”

Warde straightens. He does not draw a weapon. “You crossed the galaxy for an ending. So did I.”

Give the PCs control of the moment. They can let Hasar attack, restrain her, demand testimony, or ask Warde to answer. Hasar describes harm Warde caused without becoming a caricature. Warde does not deny it. His failure is that he treats death as the only honest restitution. A character can argue that accountability requires living with consequences.

If combat begins, use the overlook’s dangerous edges, but have Warde prioritize preventing falls rather than winning. This contradiction between his fatalism and instinctive protection is strong redemption evidence.

## The palace approach

The final miles pass through toppled statues and a road exposed by shifting dunes. Warde now moves without the cane. He may still call the building a tomb, but it is visibly a palace.

> Towers and jagged spires rise from the desert on massive columns. Weathered statues stand guard around broad steps; others lie half buried with their hands reaching from the sand. No lights burn within. As you approach, doors that have stood open for centuries widen another handspan.

Let the party choose whether to enter with Warde, scout, rest, circle the palace, or prepare a trap. Every option is valid. Scouting finds side balconies, a cliffward escape, and signs of Warde’s long occupation. Rest allows conversation but advances the Palace Awakening pressure. A trap proves the party expects violence and strengthens Warde’s fear unless designed to contain rather than kill.

Before crossing the threshold, Warde asks one final question: “If the future showed you killing me, what would you do differently?” Do not resolve his reaction. End on the answer and the sickly purple light igniting inside the palace.

## Carry forward

Record each factual contradiction to Warde’s visions, the cache discovery, the statue rescue, how the party handled rivals, whether Warde’s identity is openly known, Hasar’s status, and the answer to his final question. These become usable arguments in Session 6, not abstract bonuses detached from the fiction.

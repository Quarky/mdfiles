# Session 1 — Arrival Under a Red Sky: Full GM Walkthrough

**Campaign:** Chronicles of the Gatekeeper  
**System:** Star Wars FFG / Force and Destiny  
**Canonical status:** Repaired extended-session version, 2026-10-03  
**Repair type:** Preserves the strong v2.2.1 runtime walkthrough while adding unique world-motion, map-use, failure-forward, and carry-forward guidance. Shared boilerplate from the old scene toolkits is intentionally not duplicated here.

## Canonical runtime additions — world motion, consequences, and map handling

### What is happening without the PCs
The basin is not empty between player actions. Wind steadily fills fresh tracks; a distant engine note can be heard before the pursuers are visible; scavenging creatures retreat from both the party and the incoming craft. If Onrein Hasar or another rival force is present, they are also choosing landing cover, reading the terrain, and deciding whether the party looks weak enough to pressure immediately. Moraband itself is not “sending” enemies, but the environment makes every group feel observed and exposed.

### Run-time map notes
Use the landing basin to establish vertical cover, broken walls, and several defensible approaches rather than a single battle line. The perimeter ruins should feel like a place where the party can negotiate, hide, flank, investigate, or retreat. Keep the ship visible only on the intended landing scene. The first rival contact should have an approach vector the players can notice and react to.

### Failure forward
A failed landing/search/social check should cost time, position, strain, cover, or information. It should not prevent the party from beginning the Moraband search. If rivals gain the advantage, they may take the better route, learn the party's goal, or force a bargain rather than simply producing an unavoidable fight.

### Carry-forward emphasis
Record who saw the party land, whether the ship is concealed, whether a rival group left alive with information, and whether the party openly used the Force. Those facts should affect later pursuit pressure.

## What this session is

This is the playable story, not a preparation outline. Begin aboard the ship after Delriss and the Shard Moon, carry every injury and strained relationship forward, and end only after the landing party has dealt with the people who followed it to Moraband. The characters should finish the session with a route into the wastes, a clear opinion about the pursuers, and the first evidence that Moraband reacts to fear and certainty.

Use the material under `Prep` only when you need a clock, map zone, adversary reference, or alternate complication. The story below is the default flow.

## Opening scene — forty-eight hours from certainty

Open in the ship’s common area before Moraband appears. Do not begin with a planet description. Begin with the cost of reaching it: repair tools left out after the Shard Moon, a companion sleeping in restraints or under observation, dried blood in a deck seam, the holocron resting where nobody quite trusts it.

Ask each player one concrete question: What has your character repaired, hidden, promised, or avoided since the last crisis? Let the answers become visible aboard ship. Then bring the gatekeeper online. It studies the old coordinates and says:

> “These coordinates are accurate to the hand that recorded them, but not to the years since. Moraband was forbidden because certainty there is dangerous. I can advise you. I cannot make this decision harmless.”

The final jump uses the old coordinates. Call for the published Hard Astrogation check. Before the roll, make the stakes explicit: success means an approximately forty-eight-hour passage and a controlled arrival; failure means another twelve hours of pressure on supplies, injuries, and pursuer distance. A severe negative result brings the ship near a gravity shadow, causing the published system strain and Critical Hit. Do not turn failure into “try again.” Play the additional hours.

During the passage, run two short conversations:

1. The gatekeeper asks why each character still wants Warde’s final crystal now that they know what his legacy has cost. It does not judge the answers, but it remembers exact phrases.
2. A companion, rival, or recovering crewmember asks what happens if Warde is alive. If nobody else is aboard, let the ship’s diagnostic system pose the practical version: extraction capacity, prisoner restraints, and medical reserves are insufficient for every possible survivor.

If the characters speak confidently about destiny, the holocron flickers on a sentence that nobody commanded it to produce: “Confidence is not evidence.” The gatekeeper denies saying it.

## Arrival in the Horuset system

When the ship leaves hyperspace, give the table a moment of ordinary procedure—alarms clear, engines settle, stars regain their fixed positions. Then reveal the system: an asteroid field, seven moons, and a single dark planet beneath the weak light of Horuset.

Let the party scan before descending. Technology can identify broad ruins, terrain, storms, and the faint possibility of a small metallic object on the surface, but it cannot label Warde’s tomb. Force powers can provide a direction, but every Force roll on Moraband gains the automatic dark-side result from the planetary vergence. Describe the temptation before asking whether the character uses it: power arrives quickly, warmly, and with the feeling that hesitation was always weakness.

Read or paraphrase:

> Horuset’s light does not vanish so much as lose the strength to follow you. Beneath the ship, red deserts and black ridges stretch through permanent dusk. Broken statues lean out of the sand like witnesses trying to rise. Ruins appear on the scopes faster than the computer can classify them, each large enough to have been a fortress, a temple, or a tomb. A crimson haze lies across the world like blood suspended in the air.

The gatekeeper becomes unusually quiet. If asked, it answers: “I have no memory of this place. I have only Warde’s reasons for fearing it.” That distinction matters later.

## Choosing the landing basin

Present three credible landing areas rather than one mandatory map:

- A sheltered basin among collapsed walls: safest for the ship, poor visibility beyond the ruins.
- A high stone shelf: excellent scanners and a fast departure, but exposed to storms and pursuers.
- A dry channel near a field of statues: easier travel inland, uncertain ground beneath the landing struts.

Let the players choose. Their choice changes description and later leverage, not whether the adventure continues. A successful Piloting or Survival check secures a strong landing position. Failure means the ship lands safely but gains a problem: one blocked departure vector, dust in an intake, a damaged strut, or no clear line of fire. Advantage can identify a defensible camp or a route marker. Threat means the landing is visible from farther away. Triumph detects the distant metallic echo of Warde’s Delta-7B. Despair allows the pursuing ship to triangulate the landing immediately.

Once the ramp opens, describe silence first:

> Red dust slides across the ramp without wind. The air is cold enough to tighten the lungs and dry enough to make every breath feel borrowed. Nothing moves among the ruins. Then, from somewhere beyond sight, stone scrapes against stone—as if something enormous has shifted to look at you.

Give everyone time to act. Masks, perimeter sensors, animal reactions, scouting, and securing the ship all matter. The first clue is free: tracks cross the basin, but some are ancient and some have edges too sharp to have survived years. Checks determine what else the group learns, not whether it can leave the landing site.

## The perimeter ruins

Run ten to twenty minutes of investigation before the pursuers arrive. Give the party three points of interest:

1. A toppled guardian statue whose face was deliberately removed. Beneath it is a narrow hollow containing a corroded travel token from a pre-Rule-of-Two Sith pilgrim. It is not powerful, but touching it produces the certainty that the palace lies east—even though the physical evidence points north.
2. A campsite that appears recent until examined. Ash lies in a fire pit, a blanket remains folded, and a cup contains a thin film of water. No footprints lead in or out. This is Moraband constructing the idea of safety from the party’s expectations.
3. A ridge overlooking the ship. Fresh scoring on the stone marks where a probe or rifle rest was placed minutes ago. The pursuers have already observed the landing if the Pursuit clock is high; otherwise this belongs to an automated scout.

Allow character conversation while they investigate. The gatekeeper recommends conserving energy and finding a tomb complex. It reasons that a mortally wounded Warde would seek a place prepared for death. A skeptical character can point out that this assumes Warde remained wounded and wanted to die. The gatekeeper pauses before admitting, “That is an inference. I should not have stated it as memory.” This is the first explicit lesson in revising certainty.

## Engines over the basin

Do not have the rival party teleport onto a battle map. Foreshadow them. Sensors chirp. Dust trembles in shallow bowls. A distant engine note circles once, disappears behind the ridge, and returns louder. Give the PCs one round of unstructured preparation: concealment, weapons, a message, defensive positions, or an attempt to lift off.

Then describe the arrival:

> A ship cuts across the red sky and circles low enough to throw grit against your hull. It lands beyond the broken statues, where its engines turn the basin into a moving wall of crimson dust. A pressure door opens. Figures emerge as silhouettes first. Then a familiar voice crosses the distance: “You have led me a very long way. Do not make me regret allowing you to arrive first.”

Use the campaign’s surviving opposition. Onrein Hasar is preferred if she learned of the quest; otherwise use Nex Pavros, surviving bounty hunters, or an established enemy. Do not invent a disconnected villain merely to fill the encounter.

### If Onrein Hasar leads them

Hasar wants proof that Warde is dead—or the right to end him herself. She is controlled, exhausted, and furious that the PCs may turn Warde’s teachings into reverence. Her opening offer is practical:

> “Guide me to him. I will keep my people from your backs, and when we find what remains, you may take the device. I take Warde. If he lives, I decide whether he continues.”

If asked why, she answers with a specific wound from prior campaign events rather than a speech about revenge. If the PCs accuse her of using them, she does not deny it: “You were going whether I followed or not. I chose not to waste the trail.”

She accepts an alliance only if it includes a concrete division of authority. She refuses to surrender weapons. Compassion toward one of her injured people surprises her and lowers her hostility. Threats confirm her belief that Warde’s power corrupts everyone who seeks it.

### If bounty hunters or another enemy leads them

Their goal is capture, payment, or revenge, not ritual suicide. Their leader demands the holocron and coordinates. A hunter who has survived a previous encounter may be personally angry, but the rest still care about getting off Moraband alive. Let that disagreement show:

> “The contract says breathing.”
>
> “The contract was written before they buried two of ours.”
>
> “Then collect your grief after we leave this planet.”

That fracture gives the PCs something to exploit socially.

## Negotiation, hunt, or battle

Let the players define the encounter. Negotiation can produce an armed truce, exchange of supplies, a prisoner arrangement, or a promise to settle Warde’s fate later. Deception can send the rivals toward a false signal, but the lie will return when the rival discovers it. Stealth can turn the ruins into a hunt, with each side unsure whether the shadows belong to an enemy or the planet. Combat should move among broken walls, dunes, and the two ships rather than remain a static firing line.

After the first strong emotional outburst—rage, terror, certainty, or cruelty—Moraband responds. Shadows point the wrong way. One combatant fires at a familiar figure that is not present. Someone hears their own voice give an order they never spoke. Do not make this random damage. Make it an invitation to mistrust perception.

If the party gains the upper hand, the opponents attempt withdrawal, surrender, or a bargain. They do not fight to the last person unless an established character truly would. If the PCs are losing, the ruins can split the forces with a dust collapse, giving both sides a reason to disengage. Failure changes who holds supplies, prisoners, or the best route; it does not end the campaign.

## The session’s closing movement

End after the conflict has produced a durable relationship. The pursuers are now allies of convenience, active enemies behind the party, prisoners who must be sustained, or wounded survivors heading into the wastes independently.

As the characters prepare to leave the landing region, the ship receives one final sensor echo: a small alloy object far beyond the basin, half buried and cold. The signal could be Warde’s fighter. At the same moment, someone looking east sees a line of distant red lightning illuminate a palace-shaped silhouette that the sensors insist is not there.

The gatekeeper says:

> “The metallic contact is evidence. The silhouette is not. We should remember the difference—even if both are true.”

Close by asking who remains with the ship, who carries the holocron, and what agreement governs the pursuers. Record those answers. Session 2 begins when the landing site drops below the horizon and turning back becomes a real decision rather than an assumption.

## If the party takes an unexpected direction

- **They leave Moraband immediately:** let them. The pursuers contest the departure or follow. The gatekeeper asks what evidence would make them return. The adventure remains available without declaring their choice invalid.
- **They destroy or abandon the holocron:** Warde can still foresee them, but the gatekeeper cannot assist. Recovering or replacing the device becomes a separate problem; do not secretly restore it.
- **They kill every pursuer:** show the moral and logistical consequences. Moraband uses familiar voices from the dead later, but do not invalidate the deaths.
- **They ally completely with Hasar:** give Hasar real competence and real agency. She is not a temporary hireling; she will challenge any attempt to redeem Warde.
- **They detect Warde’s fighter from the air and fly directly there:** move the Session 2 investigation to the crash site. The false oasis and causeway become complications on the ground route after landing.

## End-state to carry forward

Record the ship’s repair state; the chosen landing site; who stayed aboard; the status and location of every pursuer; the party’s declared reason for seeking Warde; whether anyone used the dark-side result; and the first route clue. These facts must appear in later conversations and visions rather than being reset between sessions.

# Session 2 — The Search for a Dead Jedi: Full GM Walkthrough

**Campaign:** Chronicles of the Gatekeeper  
**System:** Star Wars FFG / Force and Destiny  
**Canonical status:** Repaired extended-session version, 2026-10-03  
**Repair type:** Preserves the strong v2.2.1 runtime walkthrough while adding unique world-motion, map-use, failure-forward, and carry-forward guidance. Shared boilerplate from the old scene toolkits is intentionally not duplicated here.

## Canonical runtime additions — world motion, consequences, and map handling

### What is happening without the PCs
The desert is erasing signs of Warde at the same time the party is trying to recover them. Sand slides through the broken causeway, the buried Delta-7B shifts as wind undercuts it, and distant creatures investigate anything the party disturbs. Rival searchers, if still active, are not frozen off-screen: they can discover a wrong trail, find evidence the party missed, or arrive later because of choices made in Session 1.

### Run-time map notes
Treat the broken causeway, starfighter wreck, and false oasis as distinct investigative spaces with different kinds of evidence. The wreck should reward technical and historical curiosity. The false oasis should reward skepticism and emotional self-knowledge. Do not turn every location into combat.

### Failure forward
Failed investigation never means “you find nothing.” Give the clue with a complication: damaged evidence, misleading context, a hazard triggered, lost time, or attention drawn. A failed attempt to read the false oasis can reveal that it is false while still imposing strain, Conflict pressure, separation, or a personal vision.

### Carry-forward emphasis
Record what the party believes happened to Warde, what evidence they have that contradicts that belief, and whether they are becoming more suspicious of apparently helpful visions.

---

## Opening — the landing site disappears

Begin with the expedition already walking. The ship is still visible behind them, but each ridge hides more of it. Ask who looks back last. When the vessel finally disappears, say:

> The red horizon closes over the only machine on this world that knows your names. Ahead, the ground breaks into miles of cracked stone and toppled monuments. Nothing marks a road. Every direction contains ruins, and every ruin looks old enough to have waited specifically for you.

The immediate objective is Warde’s Delta-7B, detected as a faint metal contact. If the group did not find that signal, the gatekeeper proposes searching for a tomb complex while scouts discover the same trail through tracks and architectural evidence. Choose a different character to lead each travel leg. A failed search check still reaches the next event, but the group arrives tired, exposed, separated from a useful vantage, or after the pursuers gain ground.

## The broken causeway

The first route crosses the remains of a monumental causeway spanning a dry ravine. Statues once stood shoulder to shoulder along it; now only stone feet remain. The center has collapsed, leaving a gap above darkness and a lower route through the ravine.

Give the party a real choice. Crossing the broken span is fast and visible. Descending is slow but sheltered. Searching the statues may reveal history but costs daylight. If rivals are allied, they argue for the choice matching their agenda. Hasar favors the fastest path and calls caution “another word for allowing Warde to choose the ground.” A wounded hunter favors the ravine and resents being treated as expendable.

At the midpoint, place evidence of Warde’s passage: a lightsaber-cut handhold made when the causeway was less damaged, dried fabric caught in a crack, and a boot impression protected beneath fallen stone. These establish that he survived the landing and traveled under his own power. The gatekeeper first says, “He was dying,” then corrects itself: “He had been wounded. I do not know what happened afterward.”

If the party crosses above, a slab breaks after the heaviest character passes. Let Athletics, Coordination, Move, cables, teamwork, or engineering solve it. Failure leaves a lost supply case or an injured companion, not a dead campaign. If they take the ravine, they find a wall carved with a procession of kneeling figures. One figure has been scratched away recently, and a whisper offers a character the power to ensure nobody ever has to kneel again.

## The half-buried Delta-7B

Near the end of the travel leg, the metal signal resolves into the curved edge of a cockpit protruding from red sand. Reveal it slowly: a wingtip, scorched Republic-era paint, then the shape of an old Jedi interceptor without its hyperspace ring.

> The fighter lies nose-down as if the desert tried to swallow it and stopped midway. Wind has polished the exposed hull but cannot erase the old impact scars. The cockpit is closed. Beneath the dust, one handprint has been burned into the metal from the inside.

Let the players treat this as an investigation rather than a single Computers check. Power can be restored. The canopy can be opened mechanically. Flight records can be reconstructed. Footprints and discarded medical supplies show that Warde dragged himself from the wreck. The emergency kit is empty, but one sealed ration remains because he deliberately left it. That small choice becomes redemption evidence: even desperate and alone, Warde did not consume everything available.

The navicomputer contains fragments:

- The landing was controlled until the final seconds; Warde chose this region rather than crashing randomly.
- His hyperspace ring remains in stable orbit and can be found later.
- The last cockpit recording contains breathing, a warning alarm, and Warde saying, “It happens here. I have seen it.”
- A corrupted sensor image shows a distant structure where current scanners show only dunes.

If someone repairs more of the system, play a second fragment: Warde addresses the gatekeeper prototype by name and apologizes for leaving it incomplete. The present gatekeeper becomes quiet and asks to retain a copy.

Allow the fighter to be repairable with time and parts. It cannot carry the whole party, but restoring it creates an emergency asset and a symbol that the future need not match the wreckage. If the party strips it, note exactly what they take. Warde later recognizes those components.

## The false oasis

On leaving the fighter, the route passes through rising heat distortion. Make the refuge plausible before making it supernatural. The characters see green growth in a sheltered hollow, hear water, and detect a temperature drop. Sensors confirm humidity. A thirsty companion reacts before any roll.

> Beyond the next ridge, red gives way to green. Reeds stir around a clear pool beneath dark-leaved trees. For the first time since landing, the air smells alive. Someone has set stones around a small fire, and a familiar figure sits beside it with their back toward you.

Choose the figure from character history—a lost friend, teacher, relative, or victim. Do not announce that every character sees someone different. Let them speak and discover the contradiction. The figure knows intimate emotional truths but gets one factual detail wrong. It offers rest first, then certainty: abandon the search, resurrect what was lost, or take enough power that nobody can be taken again.

Ask what each character wants to be true before calling for Hard Discipline or an appropriate Cool check. Failure means acting as if the refuge is real, taking the published strain and Conflict, but another character can intervene. Success exposes an inconsistency without instantly dissolving the scene. A character may knowingly remain because the conversation matters; treat that as a choice, not another failed roll.

Give the apparitions actual dialogue:

> “You have crossed half the galaxy for a dead man’s permission. Sit down. Let the dead speak for themselves.”

> “You call it temptation because you are afraid the answer might work.”

> “If you leave now, nobody has to know you were not strong enough.”

When someone rejects the offer for a stated reason, the oasis decays around that reason. Water becomes dust between their fingers, leaves blacken, and the familiar face asks one last sincere-sounding question. Preserve that answer for the palace apparitions.

Afterward, leave ambiguous residue: damp cloth, a recording containing only wind, or a footprint made by someone who was never there. The party should understand that Moraband can manufacture evidence as well as visions.

## The final movement

As the illusion collapses, the route ahead becomes visible: a canyon whose stone openings moan in the wind. The sun has not set—Moraband barely has a day—but the red light is weakening. The gatekeeper confirms that the Delta-7B proved Warde survived his landing. It cannot prove he died afterward.

If pursuers trail the party, reveal one of their markers at the false oasis. If they travel together, one rival secretly accepted an apparition’s promise. Do not announce this; show a changed expression, hidden object, or sudden certainty.

Close with the party choosing whether to enter Bonewind Canyon before the light fades or camp on exposed ground. Session 3 begins with the cost of that choice.

## Alternate paths

- **Repair the fighter fully:** spend the remainder of the session on salvage, defense, and a test flight. The oasis manifests around the repair site that night.
- **Avoid the oasis:** its familiar voices follow at the edge of hearing and reappear at camp. Reward caution with position, not immunity from the theme.
- **Split the group:** run the fighter investigation and oasis temptation in alternating cuts. Each group hears fragments of the other group’s conversation through static.
- **Turn back:** the route is still dangerous. Let the characters return and make a new plan without erasing what they learned.

## Carry forward

Record the state of the Delta-7B and its ring, the evidence that Warde survived, which apparition each character saw, the offer made, who accepted or rejected it, and the exact reason given. Those details are ammunition for both the Sith phantasms and the effort to redeem Warde.

# Session 3 — Night of Teeth and Voices: Full GM Walkthrough

**Campaign:** Chronicles of the Gatekeeper  
**System:** Star Wars FFG / Force and Destiny  
**Canonical status:** Repaired extended-session version, 2026-10-03  
**Repair type:** Preserves the strong v2.2.1 runtime walkthrough while adding unique world-motion, map-use, failure-forward, and carry-forward guidance. Shared boilerplate from the old scene toolkits is intentionally not duplicated here.

## Canonical runtime additions — world motion, consequences, and map handling

### What is happening without the PCs
Night changes the ecology. Predators move closer to warmth, wind makes voices difficult to locate, and the amphitheater begins producing stronger echoes as temperature falls. If the party makes a bright, noisy camp, threats find them sooner but they also gain visibility and morale. A hidden camp is safer from distant observers but gives local creatures more opportunities to approach unseen.

### Run-time map notes
Make camp placement a real choice. Give the players terrain, shelter, sightlines, and defensible options. The beasts of shadow should have an approach and objective rather than spawning beside tokens. The amphitheater should remain usable for exploration and roleplay even if the party avoided or quickly defeated the night threat.

### Failure forward
A poor camp or survival result changes the opening state of the encounter—fatigue, lost supplies, poor sightlines, or divided watches—but does not skip the night. Failed interpretation of the amphitheater still reveals a relevant echo while making it harder to distinguish history from manipulation.

### Carry-forward emphasis
Record which character trusted a vision, who protected whom during the night, and whether the party treats Moraband's manifestations as information, temptation, or both.

---

## Opening — Bonewind Canyon

Resume at the canyon entrance. The route is not merely difficult ground; it is a place that turns speech into accusation. Wind passes through thousands of round holes in the stone, producing tones like distant voices.

> The canyon walls rise until the red sky becomes a narrow wound overhead. Wind moves through the rock in long, breathy notes. At first the sound resembles mourning. Then one voice separates from the rest and speaks a name belonging to someone in the party.

Ask who answers. If nobody does, the canyon uses a phrase from the previous session. If someone answers, a second voice replies from farther ahead in that character’s own voice.

The party needs shelter before full darkness. Offer an upper ledge that is defensible but hard to reach, a ruin with intact walls but only one exit, and a low basin with water-stained stone but many approaches. Survival identifies tradeoffs; it does not reveal a correct answer.

During the passage, use one physical event and one personal event. Physically, a dust shelf collapses, an animal or companion panics, or a cable catches on carved stone. Personally, two characters hear incompatible versions of the same promise. If the party argues, the canyon repeats only the cruelest sentence.

## Making camp matter

Do not skip to an attack. Let players establish watches, light, perimeter devices, shelter, food, medical care, and conversation. Each preparation should matter later. Bright light keeps beasts at longer range but makes the camp visible. A tight perimeter prevents isolation but makes retreat difficult. Force meditation can steady the group, but Moraband’s automatic dark-side result makes the comfort feel dangerously easy.

Give one companion or rival a quiet scene. They ask a character whether the familiar face at the oasis was real enough to matter. Their goal is not exposition; they need permission to admit what they saw. If treated with patience, they warn the party before the ambush. If dismissed, they conceal fear and become the beasts’ first target.

Allow a brief period that almost feels safe. The fire burns normally. Food tastes ordinary. Then all ambient sound stops except breathing.

## Beasts of shadow

Begin with eyes beyond the light, not initiative.

> Something moves where the firelight ends. It has the gait of a hunting animal, but darkness clings to it like wet cloth. Teeth show first, then red eyes. A howl rises behind you. Another answers from the canyon wall. By the time you count the eyes, the camp is already surrounded.

Use the published phantasmal beasts, scaled to the party’s condition. If the party prepared well, they detect the approach and begin at medium range. Otherwise the first shapes are already at short range. The beasts seek fear and separation, not food. They Ensnare mobile characters, pressure the weakest position, and ignore intimidation.

Make the camp setup mechanically visible. Sensors remove Setback or expose a flanking group. Elevated positions protect one watch but require a dangerous descent to aid others. Tethered gear prevents loss when darkness surges. A protected fire can be used as a rally point.

At the end of the first round, blackness rises around everyone’s ankles. At the end of the second, the canyon voices begin issuing false tactical calls in the PCs’ voices. Ask players how their characters verify one another. A shared code phrase, physical signal, or trust developed earlier should work.

The beasts do not leave bodies. When the last is defeated or driven away, darkness withdraws and the camp contains only claw marks, scattered gear, and one object moved into a deliberate position. That object points toward an amphitheater cut into the canyon wall.

## After the fight

Give time for triage. A rival may demand immediate movement, arguing that the beasts will return. The gatekeeper recommends waiting long enough to distinguish panic from strategy. If characters used the dark side freely, they feel strong and unusually rested; tell them this plainly so the temptation has practical appeal.

Someone finds a line carved into the underside of a camp stone: THE FUTURE OBEYS THE STRONG. It is ancient, but dust inside the letters is fresh.

If the group refuses the amphitheater, the voices begin reciting private fears as they travel. If they investigate, move into the strongest spiritual trial before Warde.

## Echoes in the amphitheater

The amphitheater is open to the sky, its stone seats filled with eroded figures that resemble an audience. Extinguish or diminish ordinary light as the party reaches the center.

> The glow rods fade without going dark. Shadows detach from the seated statues and descend the steps. Robed figures rise from the soil around you, their faces empty beneath their cowls. Their voices overlap until the words become one declaration: “We are the Sith, and this is our world.”

Call for the published upgraded Hard fear check. Then pause combat long enough for the phantasms to speak. Address the character with the lowest Morality first. They know the group’s compromises because the vergence has been feeding on them.

Use specific enticements:

- To a protector: “You failed only because you lacked the authority to make obedience merciful.”
- To a seeker: “Warde’s tomb is beneath our feet. Kneel, and you will never be uncertain again.”
- To someone grieving: “The face at the oasis was an echo. We can teach you to make it flesh.”
- To someone who killed: “You have already learned that action is cleaner than doubt. Stop apologizing.”

Let every character answer. Accepting instruction or guidance carries the published Conflict but can provide a true direction or useful technique. The offer must be genuinely tempting, not a trick with no benefit.

If the party rejects them, the phantasms attack or turn the confrontation into a struggle of will if the party is badly injured. They pass through stone, reshape into personal fears, and use Willpower-based combat. Strong light-side declarations, protection of a fallen enemy, or refusing an easy killing blow should visibly weaken them.

## The obsidian stair

When the last phantasm dissipates, a sealed passage behind the amphitheater opens onto an obsidian stair climbing out of the canyon. The stairs are too regular to be natural and too exposed to be safe. At the top sits a small storm shelter built from fallen blocks.

As the party climbs, red lightning gathers on the horizon. Each flash shows an elderly man standing at the top. Between flashes, nobody is there.

If the group was overwhelmed, have the old man appear during the final exchange and disperse a phantasm with a quiet gesture. If they triumphed, he waits at the shelter with water warming over a small heater. Either way, do not reveal his identity.

His first line is simple:

> “You took longer than I expected. That is encouraging.”

End there, or allow only the characters’ immediate reactions. Session 4 is the full encounter with the Wanderer.

## Carry forward

Record camp preparations, injuries, promises made during the quiet scene, which voices deceived whom, who accepted Sith help, the exact words used to reject it, and whether the Wanderer intervened. Warde has observed enough through foresight to know outcomes and emotional shapes, but not private thoughts.

# Session 4 — Storm Shelter and the Wanderer: Full GM Walkthrough

**Campaign:** Chronicles of the Gatekeeper  
**System:** Star Wars FFG / Force and Destiny  
**Canonical status:** Repaired extended-session version, 2026-10-03  
**Repair type:** Preserves the strong v2.2.1 runtime walkthrough while adding unique world-motion, map-use, failure-forward, and carry-forward guidance. Shared boilerplate from the old scene toolkits is intentionally not duplicated here.

## Canonical runtime additions — world motion, consequences, and map handling

### What is happening without the PCs
The storm is moving across the region whether the party shelters or not. Warde has his own reason for choosing the shelter and is assessing the party before revealing anything important. He watches how they treat one another under confinement. Outside, rival groups and creatures are also forced to stop or reroute, creating a natural pause in external pursuit.

### Run-time map notes
The shelter should support conversation, suspicion, and small movements rather than feeling like a featureless dialogue box. Give Warde a place he chose deliberately, an exit he watches, and evidence that he arrived before the party. After the storm, the stone garden should visibly change the scale and mood—open air, aftermath, and the sense that a private conversation has consequences.

### Failure forward
Failed social checks do not make Warde silent. They change what he volunteers, what he lies about, or what he asks in return. Triumph can expose genuine vulnerability. Despair can make him conclude that one PC resembles the kind of certainty he once feared in himself.

### Carry-forward emphasis
Record exactly which parts of Warde's invented death story the party accepted, challenged, or disproved, and whether anyone offered him trust before knowing who he was.

---

## Opening — trapped together

Open with the storm making departure impossible. Red lightning walks across the canyon rim without thunder, and sheets of charged dust turn the open ground into a lethal choice. The old man has a stone shelter large enough for everyone but not large enough for privacy.

> The stranger braces his cane against the door until the last person is inside. Red light pulses through cracks in the stone. He passes a flask without asking who needs it most. “Introductions can wait,” he says. “Breathing cannot.”

The water is real and safe. Let the party test it. He does not take offense, but notices who drinks first and who gives their share away.

The shelter contains a heater, repaired scavenged tools, a sleeping mat, carefully stacked stones, and years of tiny practical improvements. This is not a temporary camp. Anyone who examines it learns that the Wanderer has lived in the region for decades. He avoids direct lies: he calls himself a seeker, says he came for answers, and admits he was stranded “some years ago.”

## The first conversation

Run this as a conversation with pauses, not an interrogation table. The Wanderer is friendly, brief, and sometimes distracted by memories. He knows the characters’ names or motivations imperfectly through clouded foresight. When he uses a name nobody gave him, let the silence sit.

Useful answers in his voice:

- **Who are you?** “A seeker of truth. I found more solitude than truth, but one learns to work with what is available.”
- **How did you know we were coming?** “Time reveals secrets, including those we keep from ourselves. It is less considerate about the order.”
- **Are you alone?** “I am the only living soul here. That is not the same as being alone.”
- **What is this world?** “You have a name for it. Names are useful until they begin telling us what we are allowed to see.”
- **Do you know Warde?** He waits before answering: “I knew a wounded man who used that name.”

The gatekeeper questions him precisely. Let it ask about Warde’s posture, injuries, speech habits, and lightsaber hand. The old man answers with technically true details that feel like memory rather than observation. The gatekeeper fails to recognize him and grows unsettled. It finally says privately, “He knows facts I contain but did not volunteer. Either he met Warde, or he has studied us.”

## Warde’s invented death

When trust or pressure reaches the right point, the Wanderer tells his story. Warde arrived gravely wounded. The hermit made him comfortable. Before dying, Warde predicted that the party would come. Unable to dig a grave in the hard ground, the hermit carried him to an old tomb.

Do not deliver this as flawless exposition. He stops at the word “dying,” corrects a date, and describes carrying the body with a strength his current frailty cannot explain. A medically trained character notices that his account of the wounds is exact. A perceptive character notices that he never directly says, “I watched him die.”

If confronted, he smiles:

> “Do I look like a Jedi Knight to you? I would be flattered if the comparison did not sound so exhausting.”

Allow an opposed Discipline-versus-Deception test only after players identify what they are testing. Moraband aids his concealment. Success should not print his name over his head; it reveals that the deception protects grief, fear, and anticipation rather than a plan to rob them. Triumph catches him looking at the holocron as someone might look at a younger self. Failure still leaves visible contradictions.

## The storm’s second front

Halfway through the conversation, the storm creates a practical crisis. Charged dust begins entering through a broken vent, a companion outside loses contact, or the shelter’s stone roof shifts. Warde helps without displaying his full power. He predicts where debris will fall, catches a weight a frail man should not catch, or is already moving before an alarm sounds.

This event lets the party choose cooperation, exploitation, or suspicion. If they protect him or one another, Warde Trust rises. If they arrange an “accident” to test him, warn that harming a possibly innocent old man carries Conflict. If the pursuers are present, give one of them a private exchange with him. Hasar may stare and say, “I know your voice.” He replies, “Then you know how unreliable memory becomes when fed hatred.”

## The stone garden after the storm

When the lightning passes, Warde leads them outside. Water has collected in shallow black basins between standing stones. The stones are arranged like branching paths. Several arrangements resemble decisions the party made earlier: one stone separated at the oasis, two facing each other at the landing site, one broken beside a circle representing the camp.

Warde claims he moves them while meditating. He will not say whether he arranged these before the party arrived. Let characters walk the paths, rearrange stones, or refuse the exercise. If someone deliberately changes the ending of a stone pattern, Warde becomes visibly uncomfortable. This is direct evidence that he fears mutable futures.

He asks each character one question:

- “What decision do you most wish had been inevitable?”
- “If a vision showed you betraying these people, when would you tell them?”
- “What proof would convince you that your greatest fear was only fear?”
- “If Warde lives, do you want justice, knowledge, or permission?”

Play his reactions honestly. Compassion unsettles him. Certainty relaxes him, even when hostile, because it supports his worldview. A character who admits uncertainty but still chooses a principle gives him the first model of courage he has seen in years.

## The offer

Warde eventually says he knows the tomb’s location and can guide them. He calls it only a few hours away and refuses to state his price:

> “I can take you to what remains of him. The desert is dangerous, but not complicated.” He leans on the cane and smiles. “I will ask one small thing when we arrive. If I name it now, you will mistake anticipation for choice.”

The party may accept, reject, place conditions on him, restrain him, or secretly follow. All routes continue.

- **Accept:** he departs at first light and walks more strongly than before.
- **Reject:** he gives a true direction, then follows at a distance because his vision requires the confrontation.
- **Restrain:** he allows it briefly, then demonstrates that he could have escaped. This lowers trust but does not force combat.
- **Expose him:** if they present strong evidence, he does not confess. He says, “If I were Warde, would that change what you intend to do?” The journey becomes tense and the reveal may occur before the palace.
- **Attack:** he defends, disarms, and retreats toward the palace. Move the story into a pursuit without discarding the conversation’s evidence.

## Closing image

End with the old man walking ahead beneath the red sky. His cane strikes the ground in an irregular rhythm. After several minutes, a character realizes the rhythm exactly matches the damaged cockpit warning from Warde’s fighter.

Record who trusted him, who threatened him, what each character said they wanted from Warde, and whether anyone changed a stone-path ending. Those details determine his tone and social resistance in the finale.

# Session 5 — The Long Road to the Tomb: Full GM Walkthrough

**Campaign:** Chronicles of the Gatekeeper  
**System:** Star Wars FFG / Force and Destiny  
**Canonical status:** Repaired extended-session version, 2026-10-03  
**Repair type:** Preserves the strong v2.2.1 runtime walkthrough while adding unique world-motion, map-use, failure-forward, and carry-forward guidance. Shared boilerplate from the old scene toolkits is intentionally not duplicated here.

## Canonical runtime additions — world motion, consequences, and map handling

### What is happening without the PCs
Warde knows the route and is deciding how much to reveal. The environment becomes more monumental as the group nears the tomb/palace: the whispering dunes hide old tracks, the statues create long lines of observation, and rivals can use the same landmarks to close distance. Warde may deliberately choose a harder route if he thinks the party needs to see a particular site or avoid a pursuer.

### Run-time map notes
Use the journey as linked scenes rather than one travel montage. The dunes test communication and trust; the statues externalize history; the overlook gives everyone a chance to reassess the destination; the approach should make the palace feel like a chosen threshold rather than the next map in a queue.

### Failure forward
Travel failures produce exposure, fatigue, delay, separation, or rival positioning. They do not erase the route. If the party distrusts Warde and navigates independently, allow it; the cost is uncertainty and time, not GM veto.

### Carry-forward emphasis
Record whether Warde is walking as guide, prisoner, ally, or tolerated stranger by the time the palace comes into view.

---

## Opening — following a man who knows the ending

Begin in motion. The Wanderer sets a slow pace but never checks direction. The landscape offers no trail. Ask where each character walks relative to him and who watches the rear.

> The old man’s cane leaves a line of small circles in the dust. Dunes erase your own tracks within minutes, but his remain untouched behind him. When anyone looks directly at them, they appear ordinary again.

The purpose of this session is not to hide that the Wanderer is Warde at all costs. It is to let the party accumulate a case, decide what that truth means, and prove through action that prediction does not remove choice.

## The whispering dunes

Warde answers questions while walking, but rarely initiates speech. Use the journey to revisit the campaign. He asks what became of people affected by his past decisions. If told about mercy, reform, or an outcome nobody predicted, he searches for a reason it was secretly inevitable. If told about loss, he accepts it too quickly as proof.

Give him lines that expose the argument beneath the mystery:

> “A choice can feel free and still be the only choice a person like you would make.”

> “Hope is often hindsight wearing ceremonial robes.”

> “If you prove my story false, you have not proved the future open. You have only proved I told a poor story.”

During the first hour, the dunes whisper possible endings: the party carrying the crystal over Warde’s body, Hasar executing him, the gatekeeper calling the characters murderers, or Warde leaving peacefully. Different characters hear different endings. Warde hears all of them and becomes irritable.

A storm-buried supply cache provides a practical interlude. It contains old water reclamation parts, climbing line, and a sealed medpac. Warde insists the cache is empty because he remembers finding it empty years ago. The characters open it and prove him wrong. This is small, physical evidence that conditions change. Preserve the item they choose to take; it can matter in the palace.

## Statues of the fallen

The route passes between colossal statues of Sith rulers. Most have fallen in the same direction, as though bowing toward a distant structure. One remains upright. Its face resembles whichever party member is carrying the holocron—not perfectly, but enough to disturb everyone.

Warde says, “It did not look like that yesterday.” This is the first unsolicited admission that his certainty fails.

The path divides. The obvious route passes under the standing statue, where unstable stone threatens collapse. A longer route crosses exposed ground visible to pursuers. A third route can be made by toppling the statue deliberately, which risks awakening a dark relic chamber beneath it.

Let the party solve the crossing. During the crisis, Warde’s cane snaps or is lost. He forgets to stoop afterward. If someone falls, he uses the Force openly to save them before remembering his disguise. He does not apologize. He asks, “Would you have preferred the consistency of my lie?”

This is the cleanest point for an early identity confrontation. If the party names him, he can admit only: “The man you seek and the man guiding you share responsibility.” He still leads them onward because he believes the final scene must occur at the palace.

## The overlook and rival reckoning

At the tomb-valley overlook, reveal multiple ruined complexes below and the palace in the far distance. Give the party time to stop. This is also where unresolved pursuers catch up or make contact.

If rivals are enemies, they demand the guide and holocron. Warde does not intervene immediately; he watches what the PCs do with an enemy when violence is easy. If the party offers mercy, negotiation, or rescue from the landscape, Warde’s certainty weakens. If they execute helpless opponents, his vision hardens.

If Hasar is allied, she recognizes him here. Her accusation should be personal and specific:

> “Stand straight, Warde. I want them to see the man whose ghost they have been defending.”

Warde straightens. He does not draw a weapon. “You crossed the galaxy for an ending. So did I.”

Give the PCs control of the moment. They can let Hasar attack, restrain her, demand testimony, or ask Warde to answer. Hasar describes harm Warde caused without becoming a caricature. Warde does not deny it. His failure is that he treats death as the only honest restitution. A character can argue that accountability requires living with consequences.

If combat begins, use the overlook’s dangerous edges, but have Warde prioritize preventing falls rather than winning. This contradiction between his fatalism and instinctive protection is strong redemption evidence.

## The palace approach

The final miles pass through toppled statues and a road exposed by shifting dunes. Warde now moves without the cane. He may still call the building a tomb, but it is visibly a palace.

> Towers and jagged spires rise from the desert on massive columns. Weathered statues stand guard around broad steps; others lie half buried with their hands reaching from the sand. No lights burn within. As you approach, doors that have stood open for centuries widen another handspan.

Let the party choose whether to enter with Warde, scout, rest, circle the palace, or prepare a trap. Every option is valid. Scouting finds side balconies, a cliffward escape, and signs of Warde’s long occupation. Rest allows conversation but advances the Palace Awakening pressure. A trap proves the party expects violence and strengthens Warde’s fear unless designed to contain rather than kill.

Before crossing the threshold, Warde asks one final question: “If the future showed you killing me, what would you do differently?” Do not resolve his reaction. End on the answer and the sickly purple light igniting inside the palace.

## Carry forward

Record each factual contradiction to Warde’s visions, the cache discovery, the statue rescue, how the party handled rivals, whether Warde’s identity is openly known, Hasar’s status, and the answer to his final question. These become usable arguments in Session 6, not abstract bonuses detached from the fiction.

# Session 6 — The Palace and the Unfixed Future: Full GM Walkthrough

**Campaign:** Chronicles of the Gatekeeper  
**System:** Star Wars FFG / Force and Destiny  
**Canonical status:** Repaired extended-session version, 2026-10-03  
**Repair type:** Preserves the strong v2.2.1 runtime walkthrough while adding unique world-motion, map-use, failure-forward, and carry-forward guidance. Shared boilerplate from the old scene toolkits is intentionally not duplicated here.

## Canonical runtime additions — world motion, consequences, and map handling

### What is happening without the PCs
The palace is reacting to presence through old mechanisms, unstable Force echoes, and the expectations of everyone entering it. Warde is under maximum pressure because every chamber provides evidence against the story he tells about himself. Rivals who survive to reach the palace have their own objective and should not wait politely off-screen for the final scene.

### Run-time map notes
The great hall, prophecy gallery, central skylight, balcony, and escape/exit routes need distinct tactical and emotional functions. Keep clues in the rooms where they matter. Let characters retreat, split, negotiate, or pursue across spaces. The central confrontation should not lock all doors simply because it is the finale.

### Failure forward
A failed attempt to reach or redeem Warde can still change the ending: he may flee, surrender only the crystal, reject the party but spare someone, or be defeated without being emotionally resolved. A failed combat objective should create injury, separation, damaged evidence, or a rival gaining leverage rather than forcing one scripted ending.

### Carry-forward emphasis
Record the crystal's disposition, Warde's fate and attitude, rival survivors, promises made, evidence recovered, and what the Gatekeeper is told. Those are campaign state, not epilogue flavor.

---

## Opening — a building that expects violence

Begin at the threshold with the party’s answer to Warde’s question still hanging in the air. The palace reacts to how they enter. Weapons drawn cause statues to turn their faces toward the group. Open hands cause the purple flames to retreat into their sconces. If someone carries a rescued enemy, repaired relic, or item preserved through hardship, the ancient script beneath their feet briefly glows around it.

> Torches ignite one after another without heat, filling the hall with sickly purple light. The ceiling arches more than a dozen meters overhead. Stone tiles bear letters in an ancient language, and alcoves hold robed figures whose faces have been chiseled away. Above the center of the hall hangs a chandelier made from the bones of many species. Empty corridors open in every direction. The old man walks inside as though returning home.

Do not rush directly to initiative. Let the party explore enough to understand that Warde has lived here. They find a repaired heater, water condensers, training marks, old blood cleaned from the floor, and pathways worn by one person pacing for years.

## The great hall and the evidence of a life

Warde leads toward the central chamber, but the characters may stop him or search. Present three discoveries:

1. **The prophecy wall:** hundreds of future events carved into plaster. Many are crossed out. Some failed. Some happened differently. Warde labels these “errors of interpretation,” but the evidence shows mutable outcomes.
2. **The camp datapad:** fragmented entries alternate between precise predictions and paranoid revisions. Several refer to the PCs before they were born or before they began the quest. Others predict companions who are not present, deaths that did not occur, and choices the party already refused.
3. **The mercy cache:** supplies Warde gathered for visitors he claimed he intended to kill. It includes water, medical packs, and several sizes of breathing masks. He prepared to save the people he believed would execute him.

These are not locked behind rolls. Checks provide dates, authorship, and additional leverage. The central conclusion must be visible: Warde’s foresight contains truth, but his belief that one ending is compulsory is unsupported.

The gatekeeper confronts him here if his identity is known:

> “You made me to teach discernment.”
>
> Warde does not turn. “I made you before discernment became another word for delay.”
>
> “No. You made me before fear learned to speak in your voice.”

Warde shuts the holocron off with a gesture if nobody prevents him. This is not damage; it returns when the scene changes.

## Gallery of broken prophecy

The route to the central chamber passes a gallery where reflective black panels show possible futures. Use this as the last character-focused sequence before the fight. Each character sees one desired future and one feared future. Neither should be presented as objectively true.

Ask the player what detail proves the desired future would be worth its cost. Then add the cost: someone absent, a compromise normalized, an enemy erased rather than changed. Ask what detail makes the feared future believable. Then show one element already contradicted by past choice.

Warde sees the same scene every time: himself dead beneath the skylight and the party holding the crystal. He says:

> “You think alternatives make you free. I have watched alternatives collapse into this room for twenty years.”

A character can answer with evidence gathered during the campaign. Do not reduce this to a single Charm roll. Let Warde argue. He will reinterpret failed predictions, accuse the party of arriving armed, and claim that their desire for the crystal proves his vision. Strong answers name a choice that cost the party something and could not be dismissed as self-interest.

Each concrete contradiction moves Warde from **Certain** to **Provoked**, then **Doubting**. He does not redeem here, but his opening behavior in the battle changes. At Doubting, he gives the party one full exchange before attacking.

## The central skylight — identity and attack

Warde enters the central chamber and discards the cane if he still carries it. Under the cracked skylight he stands fully upright. He shows the final kyber crystal, puts it inside his robes, and reveals his lightsaber.

> “I know why you came, even if you do not. The future is fixed, but I must still play my part. If you are worthy, you will understand.”

Let the disguise fall from the characters’ perception rather than physically changing his face. They finally see Suljo Warde in the old man. He ignites the green blade.

> “You came for my foresight. You will kill me and take it. I saw your hands around the crystal before you knew my name. I cannot change that fate—but I can make you earn it. What are you waiting for, my students?”

If Warde is **Certain** or **Provoked**, roll initiative immediately. If **Doubting**, allow one character to answer or take a non-hostile action. A credible proposal—place the holocron down, step away from weapons, offer him the exit—can delay his first attack, but not end the confrontation automatically.

## Running the battle as a conversation

Warde attacks aggressively because he wants the party to fulfill the prediction. Use Move, Enhance, Foresight, Saber Throw, Parry, and Reflect. With four or more PCs, use the additional initiative slot. He changes position constantly: central dais, pillars, side rooms, shattered gallery, and balcony approaches.

He does not attack incapacitated characters. He chooses dramatic attacks over efficient executions. Every round should contain a spoken claim that players can answer:

- **Round 1:** “You drew weapons before I named you enemies.”
- **Round 2:** “Every mercy brought you closer to the power you wanted.”
- **Round 3:** “You repaired the fighter because you feared being stranded—not because wreckage can become whole.”
- **When wounded:** “There. The vision narrows. You feel how easy the ending becomes.”
- **When a PC protects him:** “Do not pretend restraint absolves the blow you intend later.”

Allow a character to spend an action making a focused appeal while defending, locked blade-to-blade, helping an ally, or placing evidence where Warde must see it. Early checks oppose his Discipline at the published formidable resistance. The argument must cite an actual event. Generic statements about hope may earn Boost from good roleplay but cannot by themselves advance redemption.

Examples of effective proof:

- The supply cache contained something Warde remembered as absent.
- The prophecy wall includes failed outcomes.
- The party spared a rival when killing was easier.
- Warde instinctively saved someone at the statues or overlook.
- The party’s composition differs from his carved vision.
- Someone rejected the exact temptation his vision assumed they would accept.
- The PCs offer a future in which Warde lives to repair damage rather than dying to escape it.

## Dark apparitions

At the start of round two, impossible shadows pour from the side halls. Give each character a tailored vision based on earlier sessions. Call for the published upgraded Hard fear check. On failure, a phantasm manifests from that fear and enters initiative.

The phantasms argue against redemption. Victims of Warde demand death. Familiar faces accuse the party of weakness. Desired futures claim they exist only if Warde is killed. These voices are not reliable spirits; they are the dark side using memory as leverage.

Let characters weaken apparitions through meaningful acts: protect an enemy, surrender an advantage, admit uncertainty, or repeat the reason they rejected an earlier temptation. Physical attacks still work according to the adversary profile, but moral actions should visibly change the room.

If an apparition takes the form of someone Warde harmed, he initially treats it as deserved judgment. A character can point out that accepting punishment from an illusion is not accountability to the living.

## The turning point

When Warde suffers a Critical Injury, reaches half wounds, or hears two strong contradictions, he claims his death is near. This is the party’s clearest opening. If they stop attacking, add the published Boost. If they continue striking, he reads it as confirmation.

Move his state through **Certain → Provoked → Doubting → Resisting → Redeemed**. A successful focused appeal advances one step when supported by evidence. Triumph can advance an additional step or cause Warde to assist the next appeal. Failure does not erase previous progress; Warde attacks harder, the palace creates a complication, or a phantasm interrupts. Despair can move the fight to the balcony or bring rivals into the chamber, but never deletes the possibility of redemption.

At **Resisting**, Warde begins arguing with the apparitions rather than the PCs. He may add his skill or characteristic to the next social check, and the resistance becomes the published Daunting difficulty without his full Discipline opposition.

If the final appeal succeeds, describe silence before surrender:

> Warde’s blade stops close enough to cast green light across your face. The phantasms continue shouting, but he looks past them. His hand opens. The lightsaber falls, extinguishes, and rolls across the red stone. For the first time since you met him, he looks not old, but tired.

He admits that he made the party his executioners because death was easier to foresee than atonement. He gives them the crystal, but does not ask forgiveness.

## If Warde is defeated instead

If the party makes no serious effort to communicate and chooses to kill him, warn about the published Conflict before the irreversible blow. Warde eventually drops Parry, Reflect, and Center of Being and forces the predicted ending. His death should feel chosen by him, not like an ordinary depleted wound threshold.

If the party defeats but stabilizes him, the future is already disproved. He wakes furious and frightened. The redemption conversation can continue without combat, although Hasar or another rival may demand judgment.

If all PCs fall or refuse to fight, Warde stabilizes survivors and leaves his lightsaber and crystal behind. His vision failed. Footprints lead into the desert and may vanish. This is not a campaign failure; it creates a living Warde whose next choice remains unresolved.

## Balcony and rival interruption

Use the balcony only if the battle needs movement or an unresolved rival needs a final choice. The cliff lies far below, lightning strikes beyond the palace, and the wind can carry unsecured objects away. Warde may retreat here to force pursuit. Hasar may arrive with a clear shot.

Give the PCs control of the triangle. Protecting Warde from Hasar is powerful evidence but does not require forgiving him. Allowing Hasar to speak may expose truths the party needs. Disarming both sides can demonstrate a genuinely new outcome.

Do not make a single failed Athletics check throw a character to death. Use the edge as escalating position: hanging, losing gear, requiring rescue, or choosing whether to save an enemy.

## Resolution — the crystal and the living consequences

After the confrontation, let the chamber become ordinary stone. The purple flames weaken. The gatekeeper returns.

If Warde is redeemed, he refuses to become the party’s master. He accepts transport or help repairing his fighter, then intends to live among people and begin repairing harms. He can return as a peer or ally, but redemption is a first step, not erased accountability.

If Warde dies, the party finds the final crystal, his lightsaber, and the prophecy datapad. Some entries are accurate; some are impossible; some refer to future hooks. Never label which are true.

When the crystal enters the holocron, describe its faces rotating and settling. The gatekeeper says:

> “You have recovered the last portion of Warde’s knowledge. Yet the choices required to reach it have taught what no crystal could preserve. You made your own path here. Continue doing so deliberately.”

The gatekeeper’s tone depends on the ending. Redemption makes it hopeful and humbled. Warde’s death makes it subdued and cautious about teaching the final Foresight abilities.

## Leaving Moraband

Do not cut directly to hyperspace. Run the return as an aftermath sequence. Resolve the rival party, recover the Delta-7B if desired, retrieve those left with the ship, and show one consequence of each session. The false oasis is gone. The canyon voices repeat a sentence differently. The repaired fighter starts. A spared enemy helps carry the wounded. A dark bargain demands recognition.

At the landing basin, the ship should bear the consequences of absence: dust accumulation, repairs completed or worsened, anxious companions, and possibly evidence that something approached but did not enter.

As the ship lifts, ask each player what their character watches until the planet disappears. End with Moraband’s red pall falling away and ordinary starlight returning—not as proof the darkness is gone, but as proof they can leave it.

## Final record

Record Warde’s fate, Hasar’s fate, ownership of the lightsaber and crystal, the gatekeeper’s attitude, each character’s unresolved fear or temptation, all Conflict consequences, the state of the Delta-7B, and which prophecies remain possible future hooks. The adventure should alter the campaign; it should not reset after XP is awarded.

# House Dimir — Deep Dossier

## The first rule

Do not play every Dimir scene as "mysterious spy talks cryptically."

House Dimir has an overt civic history as couriers, information brokers, librarians, investigators, and archivists. The covert network works because those jobs are real and useful.

Most people need messages delivered. Most people need records found. Most people do not want to wonder whether the person performing that service is also collecting leverage.

## Cell logic

Dimir agents are compartmentalized. Orders can arrive through dead drops, codes, thought strands, intermediaries, or contacts who do not know the larger purpose.

An agent may know:

- the task;
- one contact;
- one emergency fallback;
- almost nothing about who ultimately benefits.

This protects the House and also makes ordinary Dimir life psychologically strange. Competence requires acting confidently with incomplete context.

## Lazav and uncertainty

Lazav's shapeshifting leadership makes identity itself politically unstable. A senior agent does not necessarily know whether they have met Lazav. That produces a culture where people evaluate information by usefulness, consistency, and control rather than by trust alone.

## Sensory palette

The Dimir are not always dark blue mist.

Their public spaces can smell like paper, dust, raincoats, messenger leather, ink, old shelves, coffee consumed too quickly. Their covert spaces emphasize absence: clean rooms, changed routes, doors with no sign, a chair recently moved, a message that should not be there.

## Ordinary work

A courier spends the day navigating traffic, gatekeepers, lifts, office hours, angry recipients, and weather. An archivist knows how records are actually filed versus how the catalog claims they are filed.

Those ordinary skills become espionage skills naturally.

## Knowledge as leverage

The Dimir advantage is often knowing **which fact matters to whom**.

A ledger means nothing until someone knows the name hidden in it. A love affair means nothing until a vote is close. A repair schedule means nothing until a theft needs a dark building.

Dimir plots should therefore connect mundane information rather than rely only on magical secrets.

## Why people use them anyway

Information work requires specialists. A person may suspect Dimir involvement and still hire a courier because the delivery arrives on time.

This creates the perfect Ravnican tension: everyone jokes that the messenger is a spy, then gives the messenger a sealed packet.

## Interaction habits

**Azorius:** record versus secrecy; each understands the other's dependence on information.  
**Boros:** counterintelligence cat-and-mouse.  
**Orzhov:** mutual respect for leverage, mutual hatred of owing the other side.  
**Izzet:** technical secrets are valuable, but Izzet researchers are difficult to keep quiet.  
**Golgari:** undercity routes, old secrets, territorial friction.  
**Rakdos:** Dimir secrecy versus Rakdos radical publicity can be unexpectedly useful to each other.

## How to play a Dimir NPC

Decide which layer they are on.

**Public:** efficient, ordinary, slightly over-informed.  
**Low covert:** nervous about procedure, strict about contact rules.  
**Experienced:** asks precise questions and rarely volunteers motive.  
**Deep operative:** can appear emotionally normal because paranoia has become routine.

Good dialogue:
- "I don't need to know why. I need to know whether the package changes hands."
- "Everyone has a secret. Most of them are useless."
- "You are asking who told me. Ask instead why they wanted you to hear it."

## Scene pressure

A courier offers proof that the PCs' target left the district. The proof is real. The courier's price is not money: deliver a second packet to an Azorius clerk and do not open it.

The interesting question is not whether the courier lies. It is what **true information is being used to purchase from the party**.

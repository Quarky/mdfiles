## Breadth methodology

Breadth is intentionally separate from raw frequency. It counts meaningful recurring representation across 15 categories: direct helping/protection; empathy/listening; mercy/de-escalation; personhood over category; redemption/second chances; found family/community; grief care; sacrifice/moral courage; structural advocacy; autonomy/consent; everyday kindness; outsider/nonhuman compassion; anti-prejudice/anti-collective blame; ecological care; reconciliation/forgiveness.

Category occurrence estimates overlap. One scene may count in several categories. Breadth strength is the average category recurrence among categories that clear the meaningful-presence threshold; it is a tie-breaker, not a replacement for raw occurrences.

## Zoey's Extraordinary Playlist

**Audit:** Series-spanning transcript/review scan + targeted close reads; not Full Close Read.  
**Confidence:** High  
**Modeled metrics:** ~121 occurrences · ~402 compassion min · 6.70/hour · 37% runtime · depth 91 · prominence 92.2  
**Vibe:** BROAD VIBE MATCH · common-center 91.2 · nearest Joan of Arcadia 95.5 · vibe-adjusted **92.5**  
**Breadth:** 13/15 · strength 50.2%

### Summary

Zoey can hear other people's hidden feelings as 'heart songs'. The power repeatedly turns private distress into a moral demand to notice, ask, listen and help, while the series also tests whether access to another person's feelings gives Zoey the right—or ability—to fix them.

### Why it matches

This is the clearest new extension of The Listener. Both protagonists receive involuntary access to private inner states and repeatedly convert that access into concrete help. Zoey is warmer, quirkier and more grief-centered, and the show is more explicit about the limits of over-helping.

### Major findings

- Heart songs are an episodic helping engine rather than decoration.
- Empathy changes action: Zoey intervenes because she learns what someone cannot or will not say.
- The series repeatedly corrects savior behavior; giving space, asking directly, or finding a better helper can be the compassionate choice.
- Mitch's decline makes communication, anticipatory grief and family caregiving structurally central.
- Mo, Simon, Abigail, Emily and others broaden the show into identity, depression, disability, racism and belonging rather than romance alone.
- Zoey eventually has to accept reciprocal care instead of treating herself as the permanent interpreter/helper.

### Contradictions / limits

- Heart songs are involuntary disclosures, creating a privacy/consent problem.
- Zoey sometimes intervenes badly or uses partial information as if it were complete.
- The central love triangle consumes time that could otherwise deepen the helping premise.
- The show sometimes uses the power as an empathy shortcut rather than requiring ordinary communication.

### Representative evidence

- 1x04 'Zoey's Extraordinary Neighbor'—Zoey explicitly questions whether her interventions actually help; Mo's faith/identity conflict forces her to consider method, not only intention.
- 1x08–1x09—Zoey's own emotional 'glitch' reverses the helper dynamic and shows that she also needs care; Abigail's signed heart song foregrounds agency and a father's overprotection.
- 1x11—care for Maggie and Zoey demonstrates that sometimes the compassionate response is space or a more appropriate helper.
- 2x09—Zoey realizes she has become over-dependent on powers and may miss distress that is visible without a heart song.
- Christmas special—the powers are explicitly framed as potentially existing to help hurting people, while direct communication is presented as healthier when possible.

## Twice in a Lifetime (1999)

**Audit:** Season-spanning subtitle scan across both seasons + targeted close reads; not Full Close Read.  
**Confidence:** High  
**Modeled metrics:** ~206 occurrences · ~769 compassion min · 6.10/hour · 38% runtime · depth 88 · prominence 92.0  
**Vibe:** STRONG NEIGHBORHOOD MATCH · common-center 83.5 · nearest Joan of Arcadia 90.5 · vibe-adjusted **90.1**  
**Breadth:** 14/15 · strength 52.5%

### Summary

Prematurely deceased people receive three days in their past to change a pivotal mistake. The anthology structure repeatedly turns regret into perspective-taking, repair and a second chance that must materially improve another person's life rather than merely make the protagonist feel better.

### Why it matches

Its assignment structure strongly overlaps Joan of Arcadia, Wonderfalls and The Listener: someone is compelled into another person's hidden context and must act differently. It is more overtly moralizing and spiritual than the common verified center, but conceptually very broad.

### Major findings

- Second chances are the formal episode engine.
- The protagonist typically has to understand harm they previously minimized before the timeline improves.
- Compassion is often explicitly named as a skill to be learned rather than assumed to be innate.
- Episodes cover teaching, disability, corporate wrongdoing, family responsibility, addiction, prejudice, caregiving and end-of-life issues.
- Repair often requires changing how a younger self treats someone with less power.
- Mr. Smith's arc increasingly turns repeated assignments into a durable calling to help people.

### Contradictions / limits

- The celestial court can make moral conclusions feel pre-decided or didactic.
- Three-day transformations can compress psychological change unrealistically.
- Time manipulation creates autonomy questions for younger selves and people whose lives are rewritten.
- Some disability and gender material reflects late-1990s/early-2000s framing and requires contradiction coding rather than automatic approval.

### Representative evidence

- 1x11 'Quality of Mercy'—a rigid teacher is forced to learn that teaching requires hope, encouragement and compassion, and the changed relationship alters a student's life.
- 1x14 'The Gift of Life'—a return in a disabled body challenges a character's prior assumptions and makes receiving/providing help part of the lesson.
- 2x02 'It's a Hard Knock Life'—the court grants a second chance even after serious corruption, preserving the possibility of repair without denying harm.
- 2x09 'Some Like It Not'—the assignment explicitly requires learning compassion and respect by seeing people beyond appearance.
- 2x10 'Whistle Blower'—corporate wrongdoing, disability, parental love and putting people ahead of profit are tied directly to the second-chance test.

## The Collector (2004)

**Audit:** Three-season subtitle scan + targeted close reads and contradiction pass; not Full Close Read.  
**Confidence:** High  
**Modeled metrics:** ~176 occurrences · ~616 compassion min · 6.00/hour · 35% runtime · depth 94 · prominence 94.4  
**Vibe:** MODERATE / SELECTIVE VIBE MATCH · common-center 76.5 · nearest Charmed (2018) 85.5 · vibe-adjusted **89.5**  
**Breadth:** 14/15 · strength 55.0%

### Summary

After centuries collecting souls for the Devil, Morgan Pym negotiates a new experiment: during each client's final 48 hours he may try to help that person repair the harm created by their bargain and save their soul. Redemption, accountability and costly altruism are therefore the literal procedural engine.

### Why it matches

Conceptually this is one of the strongest new matches: it repeatedly refuses to define a person by the worst bargain or act in their past. Its much darker theological/horror texture pulls the vibe score below its concept score, making it another useful example of why the project keeps vibe separate.

### Major findings

- Every case begins from the proposition that a morally compromised person may still be worth trying to save.
- Redemption requires changed action, restitution or sacrifice; remorse alone is usually insufficient.
- Morgan's own long arc turns a former collector into a persistent advocate for people the cosmic system has already condemned.
- The series repeatedly asks whether someone who has done terrible harm is still deserving of help.
- Several cases force clients to confront who benefited or suffered because of their bargain.
- Self-sacrifice can have literal redemptive force when a client chooses another person's welfare over self-preservation.

### Contradictions / limits

- The damnation framework can turn complex moral worth into a pass/fail cosmic test.
- Some clients are framed through sensational crime or pathology before context is added.
- Morgan's mission can become paternalistic because he knows the supernatural stakes and clients often do not.
- The darker violence, abuse and theological horror make the vibe substantially harsher than The Listener, Good Witch or Joan of Arcadia.

### Representative evidence

- 1x01 'The Rapper'—Morgan's bargain with the Devil is explicitly changed so he can attempt salvation for each client rather than merely collect them.
- 1x10 'The Children's Book Writer'—Morgan is forced to apply the principle that all clients are deserving even to someone he finds morally repellent.
- Season 2 cases repeatedly link responsibility and making amends to the possibility of redemption.
- 3x10 'The Media Baron'—a client becomes willing to accept eternal punishment to spare an abused child; that willingness itself demonstrates moral change.
- Late-season material continues to frame forgiveness as difficult but potentially necessary rather than as proof the harm was trivial.

## Father Brown

**Audit:** Series-spanning transcript scan across early, middle and current Series 13 material + targeted close reads; not Full Close Read. Quantitative uncertainty is wider because the run is 140 episodes.  
**Confidence:** High (qualitative); Medium-High quantitative  
**Modeled metrics:** ~565 occurrences · ~1862 compassion min · 5.10/hour · 28% runtime · depth 93 · prominence 91.0  
**Vibe:** BROAD VIBE MATCH · common-center 88.5 · nearest Good Witch 91.0 · vibe-adjusted **90.5**  
**Breadth:** 14/15 · strength 51.3%

### Summary

Father Brown solves murders, but his moral objective is often larger than identifying the culprit. He repeatedly seeks confession, mercy, protection of the wrongly accused and the possibility that someone guilty of serious harm can still repent or change.

### Why it matches

The cozy village texture overlaps Good Witch, while the repeated insistence that even frightening or guilty people retain moral personhood overlaps Joan of Arcadia and The Good Place. The murder-procedural frame creates more death and punishment than the warmest anchors, but mercy is unusually explicit and recurrent.

### Major findings

- The show repeatedly separates solving a crime from deciding what should happen to the person who committed it.
- Father Brown is willing to continue helping people after others have defined them by crime, mental illness, social stigma or prior betrayal.
- Redemption is not abstract theology; he visits prisoners, supports reintegration and sometimes risks himself for repeat offenders such as Flambeau.
- He frequently resists police certainty when an easy suspect would leave an innocent person punished.
- Ordinary parish care, food, shelter, listening and community accompaniment surround the murder plots.
- Later seasons continue the same compassion vocabulary rather than abandoning it as the series ages.

### Contradictions / limits

- Murder mystery remains the main plot engine; not every episode gives the victim or culprit equal compassionate depth.
- Religious confession can be privileged as the preferred form of repair.
- Father Brown occasionally bends law, evidence or boundaries because he believes his moral judgment is better than official procedure.
- Some viewers may find his mercy toward killers more generous than his attention to victims or legal consequences.

### Representative evidence

- 3x02 'The Maddest of All'—he explicitly frames judgment, condemnation, forgiveness and mercy as linked moral choices.
- 5x11 'The Sins of Others'—Sid returns from prison into a community that feeds, welcomes and reintegrates him rather than freezing him at his conviction.
- 5x15 'The Penitent Man'—Father Brown rejects the claim that a murderer is simply beyond redemption and continues acting as Flambeau's spiritual adviser.
- 9x04 'The Children of Kalon'—he treats a violent man's claimed reform as a serious question and is willing to support reintegration if remorse is genuine.
- 12x09 and Series 13 material—explicit compassion language and refuge/community-care plots remain active in the current run.

## Journeyman (2007)

**Audit:** Near-complete short-run subtitle scan + targeted close reads; not Full Close Read.  
**Confidence:** High  
**Modeled metrics:** ~54 occurrences · ~175 compassion min · 5.90/hour · 32% runtime · depth 90 · prominence 89.2  
**Vibe:** BROAD VIBE MATCH · common-center 88.8 · nearest The Listener 94.0 · vibe-adjusted **89.8**  
**Breadth:** 13/15 · strength 41.4%

### Summary

Reporter Dan Vasser is involuntarily thrown into the past around people whose lives he is apparently meant to change. The travel disrupts his marriage, fatherhood and work, but over the short run he increasingly interprets it not as random punishment but as a responsibility to help.

### Why it matches

This is the closest new structural neighbor to The Listener after Zoey: an intrusive extraordinary ability repeatedly reveals a person in danger or at a turning point, and the protagonist learns that the ability carries obligations. The family cost and time-line consent problems keep the compassion from being uncomplicated.

### Major findings

- Most jumps center on preventing harm or changing a life rather than personal gain.
- Dan repeatedly uses ordinary reporting skills—listening, research, persuasion—alongside the extraordinary mechanism.
- Assignments include addiction, disaster, crime, family loss, pregnancy and direct physical rescue.
- The show makes the cost to Katie and Zack visible, preventing 'helping strangers' from automatically excusing neglect at home.
- The finale reframes the phenomenon as a calling and links it to changing more lives rather than solving Dan's personal problems.
- Livia's parallel role adds sacrifice: helpers may lose parts of their own desired lives because of the mission.

### Contradictions / limits

- Dan cannot control when he disappears, so compassionate work repeatedly harms his family through absence and secrecy.
- Changing timelines is an autonomy problem because affected people do not consent to having their histories rewritten.
- The ex-fiancée/romance material sometimes competes with the helping engine.
- Because the series ended after 13 episodes, several ethical implications remain unresolved.

### Representative evidence

- 1x02 'Friendly Skies'—the assignment requires Dan to help deliver a baby while his own unexplained disappearance strains his marriage.
- 1x03 'Game Three'—he tries to warn people about an earthquake and intervenes in a life connected to addiction and later harm.
- 1x10 'Blowback'—going off-mission produces serious consequences, explicitly challenging the idea that good intentions justify any intervention.
- 1x12 'The Hanged Man'—Dan is sent to rescue a mother and son in an RV hanging from a cliff and learns that what he leaves in the past can endanger the present.
- 1x13 'Perfidia'—he helps another traveler and accepts that the jumps may constitute a calling rather than a curse.

## Method note

All numeric occurrence, minute, density, category and breadth values are modeled estimates derived from transcript/subtitle structure and targeted direct reads, not stopwatch or frame-accurate measurements. Category and A/B/C counts overlap. These scans follow the project rule that a repeated line inside the same scene is not a new occurrence.

**Numeric guardrail:** discovery evidence may prioritize a title, but numeric prominence/density/breadth values are assigned only after transcript/subtitle evidence is available.
